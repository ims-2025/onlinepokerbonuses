Poker KYC Verification: What to Send Before Cashing Out

Account verification delays more first withdrawals than any payment issue. Here is exactly what poker rooms ask for and how to clear KYC in one attempt.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published August 18, 2026·8 min read

You cleared the bonus, you booked a winning month, you hit the cashier — and nothing happens for five days. Then an email arrives asking for a photo of your ID, a utility bill and, for reasons nobody explained in advance, a selfie holding both. This is KYC verification, and it delays more first withdrawals at offshore poker rooms than every genuine payment problem combined.

The frustrating part is that almost all of that delay is avoidable. Verification is a fixed, predictable process, and the reason it takes a week for one player and a day for another usually comes down to whether the documents submitted were acceptable the first time. This is a practical walkthrough of what rooms ask for, why they ask, what gets rejected, and how to get through in a single round. What KYC is and why poker rooms do it

KYC stands for Know Your Customer. It is the process of confirming that the person operating an account is who they claim to be, is old enough to gamble, and lives where they say they live. Financial institutions worldwide run some version of it, and gambling operators — regulated and offshore alike — have adopted the same framework.

The reasons are more mundane than most players assume. Rooms need to prevent underage accounts, stop one person running multiple accounts to abuse bonuses or collude at tables, block payment fraud using stolen cards, and satisfy the anti-money-laundering obligations their own payment providers impose on them. A poker room that processes money without knowing who it is processing for tends to lose its banking relationships quickly.

Note the pattern in that list: most of it protects the player pool. Multi-accounting and collusion are the two things that most reliably ruin a poker economy, and verification is the main defence against both.

The documents you will be asked for

Requirements vary by room and by withdrawal size, but the standard set is consistent enough to prepare in advance.

  • Photo ID. Passport, driver's licence or government-issued ID card. Must be unexpired, and every corner of the document must be visible in the image.
  • Proof of address. A utility bill, bank statement or official government letter, usually dated within the last three months, showing your name and the address on your account.
  • Proof of payment method. For card deposits, a photo of the card with the middle digits obscured. For e-wallets or crypto, a screenshot of the account or wallet showing your name or address.
  • A selfie with ID. Increasingly common — a photo of you holding the ID document next to your face, sometimes with a handwritten note showing the date.

Larger or unusual withdrawals can trigger additional requests, most often source of funds documentation: evidence of where the money you deposited came from. That is normal at higher amounts and is not an accusation of anything.

Why documents get rejected

Almost every rejection falls into one of a handful of categories, and they are all trivially preventable once you know them.

  1. Cropped edges. If any corner of the document is cut off, it will be rejected. Photograph the whole document against a plain background.
  2. Glare and blur. Phone flash on a laminated ID washes out exactly the details the reviewer needs. Use natural light, hold steady, check the image is readable before sending.
  3. Name mismatch. The name on your account must match the name on your ID exactly. A middle initial you skipped at signup, a maiden name, a nickname — all cause failures.
  4. Stale address documents. A bill from eight months ago will not pass. Most rooms want something dated within ninety days.
  5. Screenshots of online statements. Some rooms accept these, some insist on a PDF download from the provider showing full formatting. Send the PDF if you have it.
  6. Third-party payment methods. A card or wallet in someone else's name will fail verification and can trigger a much longer review. Only ever fund an account from a method in your own name.

Get verified before you need to

The single most useful piece of advice in this article: complete verification immediately after you open the account, not when you want your money.

Most rooms allow you to upload documents voluntarily at any point. Doing it on day one means the review happens while you are playing rather than while you are waiting, and it means any problem — a rejected document, a name mismatch — surfaces at a moment when it costs you nothing but a few minutes. Players who wait until their first cashout discover the same problems at the worst possible time.

It also removes the most common source of complaints about offshore cashier speed. A large share of 'this room is slow to pay' stories turn out to be first-withdrawal verification running in parallel with the payment, which is a one-time cost being mistaken for a permanent characteristic. Once you are verified, subsequent withdrawals at the rooms we cover generally process far faster.

Crypto does not skip verification

A persistent myth deserves correcting. Depositing and withdrawing in Bitcoin or a stablecoin does not exempt you from KYC. The verification is attached to the account, not the payment rail. Crypto often makes the payment leg faster once you are approved, but the room still needs to know who you are before releasing funds.

What crypto does change is the proof-of-payment-method step, where you will typically be asked for a wallet screenshot rather than a card photo. Make sure the wallet you withdraw to is one you control directly — sending winnings to an exchange deposit address you cannot prove ownership of is a good way to create a second verification problem on top of the first.

Practical checklist

Before you register anywhere, put fifteen minutes into preparation and you will almost certainly clear verification on the first attempt.

  • Register with your full legal name exactly as it appears on your ID. No abbreviations.
  • Use the address that appears on your utility bills and bank statements.
  • Photograph your ID and a recent bill in daylight, all four corners visible, and save both to a folder you can find quickly.
  • Fund the account only from a payment method in your own name.
  • Upload everything on day one and confirm the room has marked the account verified.

Rooms across both major offshore networks run broadly similar processes, so preparing once covers you at Americas Cardroom, Black Chip Poker, BetOnline Poker, TigerGaming and SportsBetting.ag alike. Players in Canada should expect the same requirements as US players, with the address document simply reflecting a Canadian address.

The bottom line

KYC is not an obstacle the room invented to keep your money. It is a standard financial-compliance process that also happens to be the main thing protecting the games from multi-accounters and fraud. It becomes a problem only through timing — players hit it at the cashier instead of at signup, and then experience a routine document review as a withdrawal delay.

Verify on day one, use documents that match your account exactly, and the cashier becomes the boring, fast part of playing online that it should be. Frequently asked questions

How long does poker room KYC verification take?

Typically one to three business days when the documents are accepted first time. Rejected or unclear documents restart the clock, which is why most delays are avoidable.

Can I avoid KYC by depositing with crypto?

No. Verification is attached to the account rather than the payment method, so crypto players are asked for the same ID and address documents before a withdrawal is released.
