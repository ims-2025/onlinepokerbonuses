How Poker Bonuses Release in Increments

Poker bonuses rarely pay in one lump. Increment size decides how much you keep if you stop early, and it is buried in the terms for a reason.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published August 30, 2026·8 min read

Two poker rooms offer you a 100% match up to $1,000 with a 30-day clearing window. On the comparison page they look identical. In practice one of them might pay you $340 for a month of play and the other might pay you nothing, and the variable that decides it is not the match percentage, the cap or the deadline. It is the increment size — the chunk the bonus releases in.

This is the single most under-read line in poker bonus terms, and it is the one that most often determines what a player actually banks. Anyone comparing offers on the best poker bonuses tables should understand it before deciding which to claim.

What an increment actually is

Almost no poker bonus pays out in one piece when you finish clearing it. Instead the bonus sits in a pending balance and releases in slices as you generate rake, with each slice unlocking when you hit a defined threshold of points, rake or contribution.

The terms usually read something like *"the bonus releases in $10 increments for every 100 points earned"* or *"$5 released per $25 in rake contributed"*. That sentence, not the headline percentage, is the mechanism. Everything else in the offer is packaging around it.

The critical property of increments is that they are all-or-nothing at the boundary. If a slice requires 100 points and you finish the promotion with 99, you get nothing for those 99 points. There is no partial credit. Progress within an increment simply evaporates when the window closes.

Why increment size matters more than total size

Consider two offers with identical headline terms — 100% up to $1,000, 30 days — and a player who realistically generates enough rake to clear about a third of the bonus.

  • Room A releases in $5 slices. Our player clears roughly 68 slices and banks about $340. Only the small remainder inside the 69th slice is lost.
  • Room B releases in $100 slices. The same player completes three slices and banks $300, losing everything accumulated toward the fourth.
  • Room C releases in $250 slices. The same player completes one slice and banks $250, with the rest of a month's rake counting for nothing.

Same headline offer, same player, same volume — and a $90 spread in outcome purely from the granularity of release. The difference widens sharply for players who clear a smaller fraction, and it disappears entirely for players who clear the whole bonus. That last point is the key to reading these terms correctly.

Who should care, and who should not

If you are confident you will clear the full bonus inside the window, increment size is irrelevant to you. You end up with the entire amount either way, and you should be comparing offers on total value, rakeback and ongoing rewards instead.

Increment size matters to everyone else, which is most players. Specifically it matters if:

  • You are new to the room and unsure how much volume you will realistically put in.
  • You play irregularly — a few evenings a week rather than daily sessions.
  • You are claiming a large bonus relative to your normal volume, which is exactly when partial clearing is the likely outcome.
  • Your play is seasonal or schedule-driven, concentrated into weekends or particular tournament series.

The pattern is that fine increments protect the casual and uncertain player, while coarse increments quietly favour the operator by ensuring that abandoned bonuses leave rake on the table. A room offering $5 or $10 slices is making a genuine commitment to pay for partial effort. A room using $100 or $250 slices is not, whatever the headline says.

Points, rake and the conversion layer

Increments are often denominated in loyalty points rather than dollars, which adds a conversion step that obscures the real requirement.

A typical structure awards points per dollar of rake contributed, then requires a points total per increment. To work out the real cost of a slice you need both numbers: points per dollar raked, and points per increment. Divide one by the other and you have dollars of rake required per dollar of bonus released — the only figure that lets you compare rooms honestly.

Rooms sometimes quote this directly as a clearing rate, expressed as a percentage. A rate around 20 to 30% means you are receiving 20 to 30 cents of bonus for every dollar of rake you pay, which is roughly standard for a poker welcome match. Substantially below that and the bonus is decorative. The rake structures that feed these calculations vary by room and by format, and are set out in reviews such as the Black Chip Poker review and the TigerGaming review.

Tournament fees and the contribution question

A second layer sits underneath increments: not all the money you spend contributes to them equally.

Most rooms count cash game rake and tournament entry fees toward bonus clearing, but the weighting is not always identical, and some formats contribute at a reduced rate or not at all. Fast-fold variants, heads-up tables and certain promotional or freeroll events are the usual exclusions.

This matters because a tournament player and a cash player with the same nominal spend can be at very different points on the clearing curve. If your volume sits in a format that contributes at a reduced rate, your effective increment is larger than the terms imply — you need more play per slice than the headline number suggests. Reading the terms before you deposit

Four questions answered before you opt in will tell you nearly everything about how an offer will treat you:

  1. What is the increment size, in dollars or points, and what does one increment cost in rake?
  2. What is the clearing window, and how many increments can you realistically complete inside it at your normal volume?
  3. Which formats contribute, and at what weighting?
  4. What happens to unreleased bonus at expiry — does it vanish entirely, or is any partial progress honoured? Assume it vanishes unless the terms say otherwise.

Multiply your honest weekly rake by the number of weeks in the window, divide by the rake cost per increment, and round down. That figure — not the headline cap — is what the bonus is worth to you. If it comes out at one or two increments, a smaller bonus with finer slices is almost certainly the better claim.

The practical takeaway

Headline bonus figures are marketing. Increment size is the machinery, and it is where the difference between a promotion that pays casual players and one that mostly does not actually lives.

None of this makes coarse-increment bonuses a scam — they are perfectly good value for high-volume players who will clear the lot, and those players should chase the biggest total available. It makes them badly matched to the players most likely to be attracted by a large headline number, which is a different and more interesting problem.

Before you claim anything, find the increment line in the terms and do the arithmetic above. Frequently asked questions

What is a poker bonus increment?

It is the slice a bonus releases in — for example $10 per 100 points earned. Progress inside an unfinished slice is usually forfeited when the bonus expires.

Are smaller increments always better?

Only if you will not clear the full bonus. If you are certain to clear all of it inside the window, increment size makes no difference to your final total.
