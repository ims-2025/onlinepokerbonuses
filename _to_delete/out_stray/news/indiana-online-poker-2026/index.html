Indiana Online Poker in 2026: Laws and Options

Indiana has casinos and legal sports betting but no regulated online poker. Here is what state law says and where Hoosier players actually play.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 18, 2026·9 min read

Indiana is one of the more interesting near-misses on the American online poker map. It has a long-established commercial casino industry, it legalised online sports betting early relative to most states, and it has repeatedly come close to authorising full online casino gaming in the legislature. What it has never quite done is get an iGaming bill over the line — which leaves Hoosier players in the same practical position as most of the country, and our USA guide covers what that means day to day.

This piece sets out what Indiana law actually says about poker, why the state has come closer than most and still not legalised, and what the realistic options are for someone in Indianapolis or Fort Wayne who wants a game tonight.

What Indiana law says about gambling

Indiana's criminal code defines gambling as risking money or other property on the outcome of an event, contest or game of chance, where the person has no interest in the outcome other than the wager itself. Everything that is legal in the state operates as an authorised exception to that baseline rather than as a category the law ignores.

The authorised categories are reasonably broad. Riverboat and land-based casinos operate under licence from the Indiana Gaming Commission. Pari-mutuel wagering on horse racing is permitted, as are the state lottery and charity gaming. Sports wagering, both retail and online, was authorised in 2019 and has operated since.

  • Commercial casinos run licensed live poker rooms, so playing poker for money in Indiana is entirely legal in the right venue.
  • Online sports betting is legal and regulated, which means the state already has the licensing and geolocation infrastructure that online poker would need.
  • Online casino and poker have not been authorised, so no operator can offer them under Indiana licence.
  • Home games sit in a grey area — Indiana has no clean social gambling exemption comparable to some neighbouring states, and a raked game is clearly unlawful.

That last point deserves emphasis because it is often misunderstood. Some states carve out an explicit exemption for private social games among friends. Indiana's statutes are less accommodating, and while purely social home games are rarely a law enforcement priority, the exemption players assume exists is not written into the code in the way it is elsewhere. Anyone taking a cut for hosting is on considerably worse ground again.

Why iGaming keeps stalling

Indiana has seen serious online casino legislation introduced across multiple sessions, and in some years it has advanced further through committee than similar bills in states that eventually passed them. The obstacles are familiar to anyone who follows this area.

  1. Cannibalisation concerns. Indiana's casino industry is regionally significant and employs a substantial workforce. The argument that online play would draw revenue away from physical properties, whatever the evidence elsewhere suggests, carries real weight with legislators.
  2. Problem gambling opposition. Advocacy groups have been active and effective in Indiana, and the expansion of sports betting sharpened rather than settled that debate.
  3. Legislative bandwidth. Indiana runs short sessions, particularly in even-numbered years, which gives complex gaming bills little room when other priorities dominate.
  4. Poker is an afterthought. As in every state, online poker rides along as a minor component of a broader iGaming bill. It is never the reason a bill passes, and it dies with everything else when the bill fails.

There is a structural point worth adding. Even if Indiana authorised online poker tomorrow, the resulting player pool would be a fraction of what a serious grinder needs. Indiana's population would support a market comparable to the smaller regulated states, which have found that ring-fenced poker liquidity is thin outside peak hours. Shared liquidity compacts between states are the only thing that solves that, and joining one adds further steps and further delay. The experience of the existing regulated states shows how long that process takes.

Where Indiana players actually play

In the absence of a regulated option, Indiana players use offshore rooms — sites licensed outside the United States that accept American customers. This is the same arrangement that serves players in the large majority of states, and the practical realities are consistent.

Indiana law targets the operation of unlawful gambling rather than the individual player, and there is no meaningful history of the state prosecuting someone for playing poker online. That is not the same as the activity being licensed and protected, and the distinction matters: an offshore room is regulated by its licensing jurisdiction, not by the Indiana Gaming Commission, so the consumer protections a player relies on are those the operator provides rather than any the state enforces.

Practical considerations for Hoosier players

A few things matter more in an unregulated market than they would in a licensed one.

Banking is the first. Card deposits to offshore gambling sites are frequently declined by American issuers, which is why crypto has become the default funding method for this market. Withdrawals are generally faster and more reliable in crypto too. If you have never used it, the learning curve is a genuine cost, but it is a one-time cost and it removes the most common source of friction.

Traffic is the second. Indiana sits in the Eastern time zone, which aligns well with peak US evening traffic on the major offshore networks. Tournament schedules and the biggest cash game action cluster in those hours, so a player with a normal working day is reasonably well served — better, in fact, than players further west.

Bonus terms are the third. Welcome offers at these rooms are almost always incremental against rake rather than instant cash, which means the release rate matters far more than the advertised percentage. What would change the picture

The realistic path to regulated online poker in Indiana runs through a comprehensive iGaming bill, and the poker component would be a minor clause within it. Watching for that bill's progress is more informative than watching for anything poker-specific, because poker will not move independently.

Two developments would improve the odds. One is budget pressure — states have historically authorised online gaming when they needed revenue more than they feared the downside. The other is the accumulating evidence from states that already regulate online casino, which increasingly undercuts the cannibalisation argument that has stalled Indiana bills before. Neither guarantees anything, and the short-session calendar means progress tends to arrive in bursts or not at all.

The bottom line

Indiana permits live poker in licensed casinos and regulates online sports betting, but has not authorised online poker or casino gaming. Players wanting an online game use offshore rooms, as they do in most of the country, and the responsibility for vetting the operator falls on them.
