Ohio Online Poker in 2026: Where the Law Stands

Ohio legalized sports betting but not online casino or poker. Here is what the iGaming bills proposed, why they stalled, and what players do now.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 4, 2026·8 min read

Ohio is one of the more frustrating states on the American online poker map, and the frustration is specific rather than general. This is not a jurisdiction that has ignored online gambling — Ohio launched regulated online sports betting at the start of 2023 and it has been a substantial market ever since. The legislature is demonstrably capable of writing gambling law, the regulator is established and functioning, and the land-based casino industry is mature. Everything that would need to exist for licensed online poker to exist already does, except the law itself.

That is the situation Ohio players find themselves in as of 2026. Sports betting on your phone is entirely legal and heavily advertised. Online poker on the same phone is not licensed by anyone in the state. This guide covers what the law actually says, what the reform bills have proposed, why they keep stalling, and what the practical position is for Ohioans who want to play — including how our US poker page frames the wider national picture.

What is legal in Ohio right now

Ohio has a broad and reasonably modern gambling framework compared with many states, but the pieces sit unevenly.

  • Online sports betting: legal and regulated. Live since 1 January 2023, overseen by the Ohio Casino Control Commission, with a competitive multi-operator market.
  • Land-based casinos: legal. Ohio has four full commercial casinos plus a set of racinos offering electronic gaming, all licensed and long-established.
  • Live poker: legal. The commercial casinos spread real poker rooms, and Ohio has a functioning live tournament scene.
  • Online casino and online poker: not legal. No Ohio-licensed operator is permitted to offer real-money internet poker or casino games to residents.
  • Sweepstakes-style casino sites: contested. The direction of travel nationally has been restrictive, and Ohio's reform proposals have themselves included language aimed at shutting the sweepstakes model down rather than legitimising it.

The last two points are the ones that matter here. Ohio has drawn a line between betting on a game and playing a game, and online poker sits on the wrong side of it.

The iGaming bills and why they stalled

Ohio has seen serious legislative effort on internet casino gaming, most visibly a House bill that would have placed online casino games — slots, table games and poker — under Ohio Casino Control Commission authority, with licences tied to the state's existing land-based casino operators rather than opened to all comers. Revenue projections floated during the debate ran into the hundreds of millions of dollars annually for the general fund, with a slice earmarked for problem gambling services.

It did not pass. Neither did the companion efforts that preceded it. The reasons will be familiar to anyone who has followed this fight in other states:

  1. Cannibalisation fear. Ohio's brick-and-mortar casinos and racinos generate meaningful tax revenue and employ a lot of people. The argument that online play will eat into that revenue rather than add to it is the single most durable objection, and it persists even though the evidence from states like New Jersey and Michigan points the other way.
  2. Labour opposition. Unions representing casino and hospitality workers have pushed back hard on the job-loss question. That is a politically potent constituency in Ohio and it is not easily dismissed.
  3. Tax rate deadlock. Operators want a rate that leaves room for the promotional spending that acquires customers. Legislators want the headline revenue number to be large. Bills die in that gap routinely.
  4. Bandwidth. Gambling expansion is rarely a legislature's priority, and a bill that lacks an urgent deadline tends to sit in committee until the session runs out.

None of that means Ohio will never legalise. It means the bill has to clear a coalition problem, and coalition problems take multiple sessions. Michigan, Pennsylvania and New Jersey all took years, and the pattern is consistent: the states that eventually pass do so on the second or third serious attempt, not the first.

Why online poker specifically is the hardest sell

Worth understanding: even in states that legalise iGaming, poker is often the afterthought. Online slots generate the overwhelming majority of iGaming tax revenue. Poker generates rake, which is a much smaller number, and it requires liquidity — enough players at enough tables at enough hours — to function at all.

Ohio's population is large enough that a ring-fenced state poker market could theoretically work, unlike some of the smaller regulated states where the games barely run. But the operators making the lobbying case are not lobbying for poker; they are lobbying for slots and blackjack. Poker rides along in the bill text and then gets deprioritised at launch. That has been the pattern nearly everywhere, and Ohio players should temper expectations accordingly even in the event a bill passes.

What Ohio players actually do

In the absence of a licensed option, Ohio residents who play online poker are playing on offshore rooms — sites licensed outside the United States that accept American players. The two networks that matter for US traffic are the Winning Poker Network, home to Americas Cardroom and Black Chip Poker, and the Chico Poker Network, which powers BetOnline Poker, TigerGaming and SportsBetting.ag.

The legal position is worth stating precisely rather than vaguely. Ohio law targets operators and unlicensed gambling businesses, not individual recreational players, and there is no meaningful history anywhere in the United States of individuals being prosecuted for playing online poker at home. That is not the same as saying it is licensed, regulated or protected — it is not. There is no state regulator to complain to, no state-mandated segregation of player funds, and no consumer-protection backstop. Anyone playing offshore is relying on the operator's own reputation and track record instead of a licence, which is exactly why the operator you pick matters more here than it would in a regulated market. Banking, bonuses and the practical realities

The mechanics of playing from Ohio are the same as from any unregulated state, and they are worth knowing before you deposit rather than after.

  • Card deposits are unreliable. US-issued Visa and Mastercard transactions to offshore gambling merchants are declined often enough that most regular players stop trying.
  • Crypto is the default rail. Bitcoin, Litecoin and stablecoins clear quickly in both directions and are what serious players use, particularly for withdrawals.
  • Cheque and wire withdrawals exist but are slow. Expect weeks rather than days, and expect fees.
  • Bonuses are rakeback in disguise. The headline welcome offers on these rooms release in increments as you generate rake — they are not free money, they are a rebate on money you were going to spend anyway.

That last point is where most of the avoidable value gets lost. A player who deposits without checking the release rate, the expiry window or the qualifying game types frequently clears a fraction of what was available and blames the room. What to watch next

Ohio remains a live prospect rather than a dead one. Watch for a bill that arrives with the casino operators and the labour groups already aligned rather than opposed — that alignment, not the bill text, is the real indicator. Watch the tax rate proposed: anything at or below the mid-thirties percentage suggests operators were consulted, while anything approaching fifty per cent suggests the bill was written to look good rather than to pass. And watch whether poker is named explicitly in the authorised-games list or left to regulatory discretion, because that determines whether Ohioans get poker or just slots.

Until then, the position is stable: sports betting yes, online poker no, offshore rooms filling the gap as they have for two decades. If you are playing from Ohio, the sensible approach is to treat operator selection as the safety decision it is, use crypto for banking, and make sure you are actually capturing the rakeback you generate. Frequently asked questions

Is online poker legal in Ohio in 2026?

No. Ohio has legalised and regulated online sports betting, but no state law authorises licensed online poker or online casino games, and the iGaming bills introduced so far have stalled in committee.

Can Ohio players be prosecuted for playing offshore poker?

Ohio's gambling statutes target operators rather than individual recreational players, and there is no record of players being charged for playing online poker at home. It remains unregulated, so there is no state consumer protection either.
