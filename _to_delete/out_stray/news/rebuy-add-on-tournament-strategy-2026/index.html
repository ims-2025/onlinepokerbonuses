Rebuys and Add-Ons: When Firing Again Pays Off

Rebuy tournaments reward aggression but punish bad math. Learn when to fire again, when to take the add-on, and when to skip the event entirely.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published August 20, 2026·9 min read

Rebuy tournaments are where disciplined players quietly make money and undisciplined players quietly lose a month's bankroll in ninety minutes. The format looks like a normal multi-table tournament with an extra button, but the strategic and financial logic is completely different — and most of the players clicking that button have never worked out whether it is a good deal.

This guide covers how rebuy and add-on structures actually work, the arithmetic that determines whether firing again is profitable, how the format changes correct play, and how to keep the whole thing inside a sane bankroll. How the format works

In a rebuy tournament, the first portion of the event — commonly the first hour or the first several blind levels — is designated the rebuy period. During that window, a player whose stack falls at or below the starting stack (rules vary; some rooms require you to be at zero, others allow it any time you are at or below par) can pay the buy-in again for another starting stack. Many events allow unlimited rebuys; some cap them.

At the end of the rebuy period, everyone still in the event is usually offered a one-time add-on: a final optional purchase, typically at the same price, often for a larger stack than the standard starting stack. Unlike a rebuy, the add-on is available regardless of your stack size.

Every dollar spent on rebuys and add-ons goes into the prize pool, which is why these tournaments frequently advertise a modest buy-in and end up with a prize pool several times the number of entries multiplied by that buy-in. The variant most people mean by 'rebuy tournament' online is really a hybrid: a cheap ticket to a genuinely large prize pool, provided you are willing to spend more than the ticket price.

The arithmetic that actually matters

Here is the decision, stripped of everything else. A rebuy is worth buying when the chips you receive are worth more than the money you pay for them, measured against the prize pool the money helps create.

Two things push that calculation toward yes:

  • Chips are cheaper later. Early in a rebuy event, a rebuy buys you a full starting stack for one buy-in. As the tournament progresses and stacks consolidate, acquiring an equivalent stack at the table becomes far harder. The rebuy is, in effect, a fixed-price purchase of chips in a market where the price is about to rise permanently.
  • Other people's money inflates the pool. In a field where most players rebuy at least once, the prize pool is materially larger than the nominal buy-in implies. Your entry ticket is buying a share of a pool substantially funded by others.

And three that push it toward no:

  • Your edge has to be real. Buying chips is only profitable if you convert chips into equity better than the average opponent. In a field of stronger players, more money in means more money lost.
  • Total cost, not ticket price, is the buy-in. A $22 rebuy event where you routinely spend two rebuys and an add-on is a $88 tournament. Judge it — and bankroll it — at $88.
  • Tilt rebuys are the worst purchase in poker. Buying chips because you just lost a flip, rather than because the price is good, is how the format eats bankrolls.

The add-on is almost always correct

If there is one near-universal rule in this format, it is this: take the add-on. The add-on is usually priced at the buy-in but delivers a stack larger than the standard starting stack, which makes it the best chips-per-dollar transaction available in the entire tournament. It is also available to everyone, meaning that if you decline it while the field accepts it, your relative stack position gets worse for free.

The narrow exception is a very short stack in a very shallow structure where the add-on still leaves you with an unplayable number of big blinds and your realistic equity is close to zero. Even then the maths is often marginal rather than clearly against. If you are unsure, take it.

The bigger and more common error is the reverse — players who skip the add-on to 'save money' after a session of rebuys. That is loss-aversion, not strategy. The money already spent is gone; the only question is whether this specific purchase is good value, and it usually is.

How correct play changes during the rebuy period

Because busting is not elimination, survival is temporarily worth much less than usual. That single change ripples through everything.

  1. Marginal edges become takeable. Spots you would decline in a freezeout because of bust risk — thin value, coin-flip situations with slight equity edges — are worth taking when the downside is a rebuy rather than the exit door.
  2. Speculative hands gain value. Suited connectors and small pairs play better when you can commit deeper without survival concerns, because the implied odds are real and the risk of ruin is capped by your rebuy budget.
  3. Opponents are also looser — adjust for it. Everyone is applying the same logic, so preflop ranges widen dramatically. That means fewer bluffs get through and more value hands get paid. Bluff less, value bet wider.
  4. Do not confuse looseness with recklessness. The correct adjustment is playing more hands profitably, not shoving indiscriminately. Money still has to come from somewhere, and in a field of maniacs the profit comes from being the one who folds when beaten.
  5. Everything reverses at the bell. The moment the rebuy period ends, chips become finite and stack preservation matters again. Players who fail to switch gears bust in the level after the add-on with alarming regularity.

That final point is where most of the money is made. The transition from rebuy-period aggression back to standard tournament discipline happens instantly, and a meaningful chunk of the field simply does not notice.

Setting a budget before you register

Decide your maximum spend before the first hand and treat it as fixed. A reasonable default for most recreational players is the buy-in plus two rebuys plus the add-on — four units. Once you have set it, the number does not move because you are running badly; running badly is exactly the condition under which people rationalise the fifth rebuy.

Then bankroll the event at the full budgeted amount, not the advertised buy-in. Standard tournament bankroll guidance of one to two hundred buy-ins applies to your *actual* cost. Under that rule a player with a $2,000 roll should be treating a four-unit rebuy event as a $10–$20 ticket tournament, not a $50 one.

This is also where bonus and rakeback value does genuine work. Cleared bonus money and weekly rakeback offset the cost of the volume that rebuy formats require, which is a large part of why players in the United States and Canada who take clearing seriously can sustain more tournament volume than their raw results suggest.

Which rebuy events are worth playing

  • Big-field, low-buy-in events with recreational traffic. The looser the field, the more the format rewards a disciplined player.
  • Events where the guarantee outruns the entries. An advertised guarantee that the rebuy money is unlikely to reach means added value for everyone in the field.
  • Deeper structures. Slower blind levels give a purchased stack more time to be worth something.
  • Avoid turbo rebuys unless you enjoy variance. Fast structures compress the rebuy period into a coin-flipping contest where skill has minimal room to operate.

The bottom line

Rebuy tournaments are a good format for a patient player and a terrible one for an impulsive one, and the difference between the two is a budget set in advance. Buy chips because they are cheap, take the add-on nearly always, widen your ranges during the rebuy period and tighten the instant it ends. Above all, price the tournament at what you will actually spend.

Frequently asked questions

Should I always take the add-on in a rebuy tournament?

Almost always. The add-on typically costs one buy-in but delivers more chips than a starting stack, making it the best value purchase in the event, and everyone else is taking it.

How much should I budget for a rebuy tournament?

Set a hard limit before you register — buy-in plus two rebuys plus the add-on is a sensible default — and bankroll the event at that total figure rather than at the advertised buy-in.
