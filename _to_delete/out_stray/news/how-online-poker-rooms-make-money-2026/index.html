How Online Poker Rooms Actually Make Money

Rake is only part of it. Here is how poker rooms really earn, why they give away bonuses, and what that means for the offers you are comparing.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 17, 2026·9 min read

If you want to understand why a poker room offers a 200 percent welcome bonus, why the release rate is slower than you expected, or why the sportsbook tab keeps appearing in the client, it helps to know where the money comes from. Poker rooms are not charities and they are not casinos either — the economics sit somewhere in between, and they are stranger than most players assume.

The short version: a poker room takes no position on who wins. It is a venue, not a participant. That single fact shapes everything about how it earns, what it can afford to give away, and which players it actually wants. Once you see the model, the bonus types on offer stop looking like generosity and start looking like exactly what they are — customer acquisition priced to a formula.

Rake: the core of the business

The primary revenue line is rake, and it comes in two forms depending on the format.

In cash games, the room takes a percentage of each pot, usually capped at a fixed maximum. A typical structure might be five percent of the pot up to a cap of a few big blinds, with no rake taken if the hand ends before the flop. Because of the cap, the effective rake percentage falls as pots grow — which is why small pots at micro stakes are proportionally the most expensive hands in poker and large pots at higher stakes are the cheapest.

In tournaments, the rake is transparent and taken up front. A tournament advertised at $55 is usually $50 into the prize pool and $5 to the house. That $5 is the entire revenue from that entry, regardless of how long the player lasts or how much money changes hands afterwards.

  • Cash game rake scales with volume, not stakes. A player grinding twelve tables of micro stakes can generate more rake than one playing a single mid-stakes table.
  • Tournament rake is collected once. A re-entry event collects it again on each bullet, which is part of why re-entry formats proliferated.
  • Rakeback is a rebate, not a bonus. It returns a share of the rake you already paid, which makes it fundamentally different from a deposit match.
  • The cap matters more than the percentage. Two rooms advertising the same rate can charge very different amounts depending on where the cap sits.

This is the critical insight for anyone comparing offers: the room's revenue from you is a function of how much you play, not whether you win. A winning player who plays enormous volume is a better customer than a losing player who plays occasionally. That is genuinely different from a casino, and it explains behaviour that otherwise looks irrational.

Why bonuses exist at all

If rake is the revenue, a welcome bonus is a marketing expense — and like any marketing expense, it is budgeted against expected lifetime value.

The arithmetic is not complicated. A room estimates what an average new depositor will generate in rake over their first year. It then decides what share of that it is willing to spend to acquire them. The welcome offer is sized to that figure and structured so that the payout is conditional on the player actually generating the rake that justifies it.

This is why almost every meaningful poker bonus releases incrementally against rake rather than arriving as cash. The room is not being stingy. It is making the marketing spend self-funding — you receive the bonus precisely in proportion to the revenue you have already produced. A bonus that paid out instantly with no requirement would be a pure loss on every player who took it and left, and rooms that have tried it have been comprehensively exploited.

It also explains the headline percentage inflation. A 200 percent match with a slow release schedule costs the room less in practice than a 100 percent match that releases quickly, because a much smaller share of it ever gets claimed. The advertised number is a marketing input; the release rate is the actual price. Our best poker bonuses rankings weight the two accordingly rather than sorting by headline size.

The other revenue streams

Rake is the core, but for most offshore operators serving American players it is not the whole picture — and in some cases it is not even the largest line.

  1. The casino and sportsbook. Nearly every room we cover sits inside a broader gambling platform. The margin on slots and sports betting is considerably higher than on poker, and a poker player who occasionally visits the casino tab is far more profitable than one who does not.
  2. Payment processing spreads. Where deposits and withdrawals pass through crypto conversion, small spreads and fees accumulate across a large volume of transactions.
  3. Fees on specific actions. Withdrawal fees beyond a free monthly allowance, inactivity fees on dormant accounts, and currency conversion charges all contribute.
  4. Side products. Fast-fold formats, lottery-style sit and gos and similar variants often carry a higher effective rake than standard games while feeling faster and more casual.

The cross-sell point deserves particular attention because it shapes the products you see. When a poker client pushes casino promotions, bundles sportsbook credit into a poker offer, or runs a promotion that requires a small casino wager, that is not a distraction from the business model — it is the business model. Poker acquires a customer at modest margin; the casino monetises them at a much higher one. Where affiliates fit

Worth being direct about this, since you are reading it on a site that operates in this space. Poker rooms pay affiliates to send them players, usually either as a share of the revenue that player generates or as a one-off payment per depositing customer.

The implications are worth understanding as a reader. An affiliate earning revenue share has an interest in you having a good long-term experience, because a player who quits in frustration generates nothing. An affiliate paid per signup does not. Bonus codes distributed through affiliates sometimes carry better terms than the room's default offer, because the affiliate has negotiated an enhanced deal as part of the arrangement — which is a real benefit rather than a trick, but only if the underlying room is one worth playing at.

What this means for the player

Several practical conclusions follow from the model, and they are more useful than most bonus advice.

First, the room is indifferent to your results but highly sensitive to your volume. This means you are never penalised for winning, but it also means every promotional structure is designed to increase how much you play. Recognise that pull and decide deliberately rather than being drawn along by a progress bar.

Second, rake is your largest fixed cost and the one players think about least. Over a year of meaningful volume, the difference between a favourable and an unfavourable rake structure will exceed any welcome bonus you were comparing. Ongoing rakeback and loyalty rewards therefore matter more than the one-time offer that got you through the door.

Third, the cap structure and the ongoing rewards programme tell you more about a room than the banner does. Rooms that pay generously on an ongoing basis are making a bet on long-term players; rooms with enormous headline offers and thin ongoing rewards are optimised for churn. Both exist among the operators serving US and Canadian players, and the difference is visible if you look at the second page of the terms rather than the first.

The bottom line

Online poker is a volume business selling a service — a game, a lobby, a payment system — to players who pay for it in rake. Bonuses are the acquisition cost of that business, priced against expected lifetime rake and released in proportion to it. The casino and sportsbook alongside the poker client are where the higher margins live, which is why they are never far from view.

None of that makes the offers bad. A welcome bonus you can realistically clear is real money and worth claiming. But it is a negotiated transaction rather than a gift, and players who understand the terms of that transaction consistently extract more from it. Compare release rates rather than headline percentages, weigh ongoing rewards against one-time offers, and read the terms before the deposit rather than after — the best poker bonuses rankings are built on exactly that basis.

Frequently asked questions

How do online poker rooms make money if they don't play?

They take rake — a capped percentage of each cash game pot, or a fee added to each tournament buy-in — plus revenue from attached casino and sportsbook products.

Why do poker bonuses release slowly instead of paying instantly?

Because the bonus is a marketing cost funded by the rake you generate. Incremental release ensures the room only pays out in proportion to revenue already earned.
