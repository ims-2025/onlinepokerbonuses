Short-Stack Strategy: Playing 20 Big Blinds or Less

Twenty big blinds changes poker completely. Learn the push-fold shift, which hands gain value, and how short stacks affect bonus clearing and bankroll.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published August 28, 2026·9 min read

Somewhere below twenty big blinds, poker stops being the game most players think they are playing. Post-flop skill, the thing recreational players spend most of their study time on, becomes almost irrelevant. Position matters less. Implied odds effectively vanish. What replaces all of it is a much narrower, much more solvable problem: given this stack, this position and these blinds, do I put it all in or do I fold?

That sounds like a downgrade, and for players who enjoy deep-stacked poker it often feels like one. But short-stack play is where an enormous proportion of tournament equity is won and lost, and it is also the situation most players handle worst — partly because it arrives when they are already frustrated, and partly because the correct strategy is genuinely counterintuitive. Why twenty big blinds is the boundary

The threshold is not arbitrary. It is the point at which the standard three-bet-and-continue structure of a hand stops fitting inside your stack.

With a hundred big blinds, a raise, a three-bet, a call, and then flop, turn and river betting all fit comfortably. Every street is a decision, and skill accumulates across all of them. Drop to twenty and the arithmetic collapses: a standard open of two and a half big blinds followed by a three-bet to eight leaves you committed with twelve behind on a stack that started at twenty. The three-bet was effectively an all-in wearing a disguise. Below fifteen it stops being a disguise at all.

This is why the transition is sharp rather than gradual. You are not playing a slightly compressed version of deep-stacked poker. You are playing a different game whose main decision — shove or fold — has a mathematically correct answer far more often than deep-stacked spots do.

What changes about hand values

The hands that gain and lose value at short stacks surprise players who have internalised full-stack ranges.

  • Ace-rag gains enormously. A hand like A5 offsuit is a marginal, easily dominated holding at 100 big blinds. At 12 it is a strong shove from late position: it blocks the best calling hands, and it wins outright a large share of the time by making everyone fold.
  • Small pairs lose relative value. Pocket fives is a set-mining hand deep, and set-mining requires implied odds that no longer exist. It remains playable but as a coinflip-seeking shove rather than a speculative call.
  • Suited connectors fall hardest. 76 suited is built entirely on implied odds and post-flop manoeuvring. Strip both away and it is a weak hand with modest all-in equity.
  • Any two broadway cards rise. KJ, QT and similar hands do well against calling ranges and fold out plenty of equity when shoved.
  • Suitedness matters less than you think. It adds roughly two to four percent equity in an all-in — real, but not enough to promote a hand you would otherwise fold.

The unifying principle is that short-stack hand value is driven by two things only: fold equity and all-in equity against a calling range. Playability, the quality that makes suited connectors attractive deep, contributes essentially nothing.

The push-fold framework in practice

You do not need to memorise a chart to play short stacks competently, though charts exist and are worth studying. What you need is the reasoning behind them, because the reasoning transfers to spots the charts do not cover.

Every shove is really two bets in one. The first is a bet that everyone folds, which wins you the blinds and antes immediately. The second is a bet that if someone calls, your hand holds up often enough to justify the risk. The relative weight of those two bets shifts with position and with how many players remain to act.

  1. From late position with few players behind, fold equity dominates. Your range widens dramatically — from the button at ten big blinds, a very large share of all hands is a profitable shove, because the two players behind fold most of the time.
  2. From early position at a full table, fold equity is weak because many players can wake up with a hand. Your range tightens toward holdings that perform acceptably when called.
  3. Against a short stack in the blinds, fold equity drops further — a player with eight big blinds calls far wider than one with forty, because they are already committed.
  4. Against a large stack, calls get looser too, since the big stack risks a small fraction of their chips. Adjust toward genuine equity rather than pure steal attempts.
  5. When antes are in play, everything widens. The dead money in the middle improves the price on winning the pot uncontested, which is why ante levels are where short-stack aggression pays best.

The single most common leak is not shoving too wide. It is shoving too tight from late position — folding hands that are clearly profitable because they feel weak, and blinding down from twelve big blinds to six while waiting for a hand that never arrives. Every orbit you fold costs you a meaningful percentage of your stack, and a stack that reaches five big blinds has lost almost all its fold equity. Acting at twelve is worth far more than acting perfectly at five.

Calling a shove is harder than making one

Shoving ranges are wide; calling ranges are not, and confusing the two is expensive. When you shove you can win two ways. When you call you can only win by having the better hand at showdown.

That asymmetry means a hand you would happily shove from the button is often a clear fold when facing a shove from the same position. Ace-eight offsuit shoved at twelve big blinds is routine; ace-eight offsuit calling a twelve-big-blind shove from a tight player is frequently a losing proposition. The gap between the two ranges is wider than intuition suggests, and it widens further in tournaments where finishing position carries real money implications — the same ICM pressure that shapes final-table decisions applies at every stage where payouts loom.

Short stacks, session length and bonus clearing

There is a practical dimension to this that pure strategy content tends to skip. How you play short stacks determines how long your tournament sessions last, and session length determines rake generation — which determines how quickly a pending bonus releases.

A player who blinds down passively busts earlier and generates less volume than one who takes the correct aggressive line, because rebuy and re-entry decisions aside, deeper runs mean more hands, more rake and more accumulated points. That is not a reason to play badly for volume — spewing chips to stay in pots is a terrible trade — but it is a reason to note that correct short-stack play and efficient bonus clearing point in the same direction. The bankroll implication runs the same way. Short-stack play is high-variance by construction: you are taking coinflips and slight edges repeatedly rather than grinding small post-flop advantages. Playing it correctly means accepting more swings for a higher expectation, which only works if your bankroll can absorb the swings without forcing you down in stakes at the wrong moment.

Building the skill efficiently

Short-stack play is the most trainable part of poker, because the decision space is small enough to be studied exhaustively. A player who spends a few focused hours on push-fold ranges will improve their tournament results more than one who spends the same hours on river bluffing frequencies — simply because the short-stack spots come up several times per tournament and the river spots do not.

Start with late-position shoving ranges at ten to fifteen big blinds, which is where the most equity sits and where the widest gap exists between typical play and correct play. Then work backwards to calling ranges, which are the more expensive half to get wrong. Review your own busts specifically: the hand where you folded at eleven big blinds and shoved at six is a mistake that will not appear in any hand-history filter for big pots, because you never got the chips in.

Frequently asked questions

At what stack size should I switch to push-fold poker?

Around twenty big blinds the standard raise-and-continue structure stops fitting your stack, and below roughly fifteen most spots reduce cleanly to a shove-or-fold decision.

Why is ace-five better than a small pair at short stacks?

Ace-five blocks the strongest calling hands and wins often without a showdown, while small pairs rely on set-mining value that no longer exists once implied odds disappear.
