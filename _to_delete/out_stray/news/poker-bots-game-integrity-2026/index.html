Poker Bots in 2026: How Sites Protect Your Games

Bots are the biggest threat to online poker's credibility. Here is how rooms detect them, what they refund, and the warning signs to watch for.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published August 22, 2026·9 min read

Ask a group of online poker players what worries them most and bots will come up before rake, before bonus terms, before withdrawal times. It is the one concern that goes to the foundation of the product: if you cannot trust that the seats around you contain humans, nothing else about the site matters.

The concern is legitimate but frequently misunderstood. Bots exist, rooms take them seriously, detection has improved substantially, and the practical risk to a recreational player at small and mid stakes is smaller than forum panic suggests. What follows is a realistic account of the problem, what operators actually do about it, and how to evaluate a room's integrity record before you deposit. It is one of the factors we weigh when compiling our best poker bonuses rankings, because a generous offer at an unpoliced site is worth nothing.

What a poker bot actually is

A bot is software that plays hands without a human making the decisions. That covers a wide range of sophistication, and the distinction matters when you are assessing risk.

  • Simple rule-based bots follow fixed instructions — fold this, raise that. They are cheap to build, easy to detect, and beat almost nobody who is paying attention.
  • Solver-driven bots play a pre-computed strategy that is difficult to exploit. These are the serious ones, and they are expensive to build and operate.
  • Real-time assistance (RTA) is a human clicking the buttons while software tells them what to do. Technically not a bot, treated identically by every room's terms, and harder to detect because the timing looks human.
  • Bot farms run many accounts in coordination, sometimes sharing hole-card information between seats at the same table. This is the most damaging category because it combines automation with collusion.

The economics matter here. A sophisticated bot operation is a real investment — development, infrastructure, accounts, and the constant risk of losing balances to confiscation. That investment only pays back at stakes where the money is meaningful. It is why the overwhelming majority of confirmed cases have surfaced in mid and high-stakes games rather than in the micro-stakes and low-stakes pools where most recreational players live.

How rooms detect them

Detection is not one technique but a stack of overlapping ones, and no operator publishes the details, for the obvious reason that doing so would be a manual for evading them. In broad terms, security teams look at:

  1. Timing analysis. Humans are inconsistent. They take four seconds on a hard decision and half a second on an obvious fold, they slow down when tired, they occasionally time out. Automated play produces timing distributions that are subtly too regular, and this remains one of the strongest signals available.
  2. Strategic fingerprinting. Comparing a player's decisions against known solver outputs. A human approximates a strategy; software reproduces it with a consistency that is statistically implausible over large samples.
  3. Client and device forensics. Detecting software running alongside the poker client, virtual machine signatures, input patterns and hardware fingerprints.
  4. Account and network correlation. Shared IP ranges, funding sources, device identifiers and login patterns across accounts — the main tool against farms rather than individuals.
  5. Hand-history review by humans. Automated flags get reviewed by analysts before action is taken, because false positives cost real money and real reputation.
  6. Player reports. Still valuable. Security teams cannot watch every table, and a specific, well-documented report from a player who has seen something strange gives them a place to look.

The arms race is genuine and permanent. Detection improves, evasion adapts, detection improves again. No operator will ever declare the problem solved, and any that did should be treated with suspicion.

What happens when a bot is caught

The standard response across the major US-facing rooms is confiscation and redistribution: the offending account is closed, its balance seized, and the funds distributed back to players who lost money at its tables. Some operators publish periodic reports of these actions.

That publication practice is worth paying attention to as a consumer signal. A room that announces bans, names the number of accounts closed and states how much was returned to players is a room whose security team has something to show. Silence is ambiguous — it might mean nothing is happening, or it might mean nothing is being found — but consistent, specific transparency is one of the few externally visible indicators of an active integrity programme. Both major networks serving this market have run publicised enforcement efforts, and the Americas Cardroom and BetOnline Poker reviews cover how each handles game security.

Refunds are typically calculated from the money the flagged account won at tables where you were seated, over the period the account was active. Do not expect compensation for hands you were not involved in, and do not expect the process to be fast — investigations take time precisely because doing them properly requires human review.

Structural defences that help

Some of the most effective anti-bot measures are not detection at all — they are design choices that make automated play less profitable in the first place.

Anonymous tables are the biggest one. A bot that cannot identify opponents cannot target weak players, cannot avoid other bots, and cannot build the opponent database that makes an exploitative strategy pay. Anonymity flattens the advantage of both bots and heavy trackers simultaneously.

Seating restrictions and randomised table assignment prevent the auto-seating scripts that bot operations rely on to fill profitable seats quickly. Table limits per account cap how much volume any single operator can push through. Mandatory client updates close the technical gaps that third-party automation exploits. Verification requirements before cashout add friction to the withdrawal step, which is where an operation running dozens of accounts is most exposed — a point covered in more depth in our how to claim guide, since the same verification process applies to your own first withdrawal.

What you can realistically watch for

You are not going to identify a well-built bot from the other side of a table, and you should be sceptical of anyone who claims they routinely can. Most accusations levelled in chat boxes are aimed at players who simply played a hand differently than expected. That said, some patterns are worth noting and reporting:

  • Metronomic timing. Near-identical decision times across wildly different situations, sustained over hundreds of hands.
  • Marathon sessions without breaks. Twenty-plus hours of continuous play at multiple tables with no variation in tempo.
  • Rigid, unvarying bet sizing that never adjusts to opponent, texture or history.
  • Total non-interaction. Never responds to chat, never reacts to anything unusual at the table. Weak evidence alone; meaningful alongside the others.
  • Coordinated behaviour between accounts. Two players who consistently avoid confronting each other or who arrive and leave tables together. This is the pattern most worth reporting.

If something looks wrong, send support the table ID, the approximate time, the screen names involved and a short description of what you observed. Specific reports get investigated; vague ones do not. And keep expectations calibrated — you will rarely be told the outcome, because operators do not discuss individual accounts with other players.

How much should this affect where you play

Game integrity belongs on your checklist, but it should sit alongside payout reliability and terms transparency rather than dominating them. The order of practical concerns for most players is: does the room pay reliably, are the bonus terms honest, is there enough traffic in your games, and does the operator show evidence of active security work.

The reassuring reality is that the stakes most people play are not economically attractive to serious bot operations, and the structural defences that have become standard — anonymity, seating controls, verification — hit the low-effort operations hardest. The unreassuring reality is that this is a permanent arms race and no room is immune. Choosing an operator with a long track record and visible enforcement activity is the most useful thing within your control.

The bottom line

Bots are a real and permanent feature of online poker's threat landscape, not a myth and not an excuse for a bad session. Rooms that take them seriously invest in layered detection, publish enforcement results and design their games to make automation unprofitable. Rooms that do not, eventually lose their player base.

Frequently asked questions

Are poker bots common at low-stakes online tables?

Sophisticated bot operations are expensive to run, so they concentrate at stakes where the returns justify the investment. Low-stakes games see far fewer confirmed cases than mid and high-stakes pools.

Do I get my money back if I played against a bot?

Major rooms typically confiscate the offending account's balance and redistribute it to affected players, based on losses at tables where the account was active. Investigations take time and refunds are not guaranteed.
