Hand Reading: How to Put Opponents on a Range

Stop guessing single hands. Learn how to build and narrow an opponent's range street by street, the skill that separates winners from breakeven grinders.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 20, 2026·9 min read

Ask a losing player what their opponent has and you will usually get a single hand: "he's got ace-king." Ask a winning player the same question and you get a list — a collection of hands weighted by likelihood, which shrinks as the hand progresses. That difference in framing is the single largest gap between a player who breaks even and one who beats the game. Everything else — bet sizing, bluff selection, when to fold a good-looking hand — sits downstream of it. It also happens to be the skill that makes the volume you put in while clearing a poker bonus actually profitable rather than merely fast.

Hand reading sounds mystical when it is described as reading souls across a felt. Online, where you have no physical tells at all, it is something far more mechanical: a process of elimination run against the bets your opponent chose to make. Here is how that process works, and how to run it fast enough to use at a real table.

Start with the preflop range, not the flop

The hand-reading process begins before a single community card appears, and most players skip this step entirely. When an opponent opens from middle position, they are not opening one hand — they are opening some percentage of all possible hands, and that percentage is knowable.

A typical online regular opens roughly 15–18% of hands from middle position, 22–26% from the cutoff, and 40%+ from the button. A loose recreational player might open 35% from every seat. Those numbers are your starting point. You do not need to memorise exact charts; you need to know whether you are facing a narrow range or a wide one, because every later deduction is a subtraction from that starting set.

  • Position defines width. Earlier position means a tighter, stronger range. A raise from under the gun has far fewer weak suited hands in it than the same raise from the button.
  • Action defines strength. An open is wide; a 3-bet is narrow; a cold 4-bet is very narrow. Each additional raise before the flop cuts the range dramatically.
  • Player type shifts everything. A tight-passive player's 3-betting range might be six hands. An aggressive regular's might be forty, half of them bluffs.
  • Stack depth matters. Short stacks open and shove wider ranges of high-card hands; deep stacks include more speculative suited and connected holdings.

If you do nothing else, get in the habit of saying a rough preflop range to yourself before the flop lands. "Cutoff open, so pairs, broadways, suited aces, suited connectors, some offsuit broadways." That sentence takes two seconds and makes the rest of the hand tractable.

Use the flop to subtract, not to guess

When the flop comes, the mistake is to ask "what does this flop give him?" The better question is "which parts of his preflop range connect with this board, and how does his bet size sort them?"

Take a cutoff opener facing a big blind caller on a board of K-7-2 rainbow. The opener's range contains a great many hands that missed entirely — suited connectors, small pairs, suited aces without an ace-high pair. It contains a modest number of kings. It contains a large block of ace-high and broadway hands with backdoor equity. When that player fires a small continuation bet, they are betting most of that range, which means the bet tells you almost nothing — and that is itself information.

Contrast that with a board of J-T-8 two-tone. Now the opener's range interacts far more densely: straights, two pair, huge draws, plus a lot of overcards that are now in genuine trouble. A large bet on that board is a very different message from a large bet on K-7-2, because the range being bet is shaped differently. The same bet size carries opposite meanings on different flops, and the board is what decides which meaning applies.

The working rule: small bets on dry boards mean wide ranges; large bets on connected boards mean polarised ones. A polarised range is very strong hands and bluffs with little in between.

The turn is where ranges actually narrow

Most of the real information in a poker hand arrives on the turn, because the turn is the first street where players have to commit meaningful money with a range they cannot bet entirely.

A player who continuation bets the flop with 70% of their range cannot profitably fire again with 70% on the turn. The pot is larger, the bet is larger, and the bluffs that made sense with two cards to come stop making sense with one. So when the second barrel comes, ask what hands could logically still be betting. Usually the answer is: strong made hands, the best draws, and a small number of well-chosen bluffs with blockers.

Equally important is the turn *check*. A player who bet the flop and then checks the turn has often given up with a hand that cannot call a bet, or slowed down with a medium-strength hand they want to showdown cheaply. Both are exploitable, and both are invisible if you are not tracking the range.

  1. Ask what the turn card changed. Did it complete an obvious draw, put a second overcard out, or pair the board? The card matters less than what it does to each player's range.
  2. Ask whether the bet size shifted. A player who c-bet small on the flop and suddenly fires two-thirds pot on the turn has usually improved or is representing improvement.
  3. Ask what hands would check here. The hands your opponent would *not* bet are as informative as the ones they would.
  4. Carry the read forward. The range you build on the turn is the range you face on the river. Do not reset.

River decisions are range-versus-range

By the river, if you have been tracking properly, your opponent's range should be down to a handful of combinations. Now the question becomes arithmetic rather than intuition: of the hands still in their range, how many beat you and how many do not?

This is where combinatorics earns its keep. If your opponent's river range is "sets and missed flush draws," count them. There are three combinations of each specific set; there may be nine or twelve combinations of missed flush draws depending on which cards you hold. If the bluffs outnumber the value hands and the price is right, you call — regardless of how uncomfortable the board looks. The cards in your own hand matter too, because they remove combinations from your opponent's range, which is the whole basis of blocker-based decisions.

A call is not a guess about one hand. It is a bet that your equity against the whole remaining range beats the pot odds you are being offered.

Building the skill without slowing down

Hand reading is slow at first, which is why most players abandon it mid-session and fall back on gut feel. The fix is to practise it away from the table until the process compresses.

  • Review hands you were not in. Watching a hand without money at stake lets you narrate the range out loud at full speed with no pressure.
  • Play fewer tables while learning. You cannot run this process on twelve tables. Drop to two, build the habit, then scale back up.
  • Narrate one hand per orbit. You do not need to hand-read every pot. Pick one and do it properly; the pattern recognition generalises.
  • Focus on your regular opponents. In a fixed player pool, ten well-understood opponents are worth more than a vague model of everyone.

It also helps to play where the ranges are readable. Anonymous tables strip out the history that makes hand reading efficient, while rooms with visible screen names and stable player pools reward it heavily. The bottom line

Hand reading is not a psychic skill. It is a disciplined habit of assigning a range preflop and subtracting from it on every street based on the actions your opponent takes and the hands those actions make sense with. Do it consistently and your bluff-catches, your value bets, and your folds all improve at once, because they are all downstream of the same question.

Frequently asked questions

What does putting someone on a range mean?

It means assigning your opponent a set of possible hands rather than one specific holding, then narrowing that set as they act on each street.

Can you hand read without a HUD or tracking software?

Yes. Position, bet sizing, and board texture supply most of the information. Statistics speed up the profiling of unknown opponents but are not required.
