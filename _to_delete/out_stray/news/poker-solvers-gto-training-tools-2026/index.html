Poker Solvers and GTO Training Tools in 2026

Solvers changed how poker is studied, not how it is played. Here is what GTO tools actually do, where they help, and where they quietly cost you money.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 20, 2026·9 min read

A decade ago, solver output was the preserve of a few high-stakes players with the hardware and patience to run it. Today a competent solver runs in a browser, preloaded solutions cover most common spots, and any player willing to spend a modest monthly fee can look up a near-optimal strategy for almost any situation they will face online. The tools have democratised completely — and the interesting question is no longer whether to use them, but what they actually do for the player sitting in a $25 tournament or a low-stakes cash game funded by a welcome bonus.

The short answer: solvers are an extraordinary study aid and a poor strategy, and the difference between those two things is where most players lose money.

What a solver actually computes

A poker solver takes a defined situation — stack sizes, board, ranges for both players, available bet sizes — and iterates toward a Nash equilibrium. That equilibrium is a strategy pair where neither player can improve by unilaterally changing what they do. It is genuinely unexploitable in the game-theoretic sense.

Three things about that output are routinely misunderstood.

  • It is unexploitable, not maximally profitable. An equilibrium strategy guarantees you cannot be beaten badly. It does not extract the most money from a player making obvious mistakes — and at low and mid stakes, most players are.
  • It depends entirely on the inputs. Change the assumed preflop range or remove a bet size from the tree and the output changes, sometimes dramatically. A solution is only as good as the assumptions fed into it.
  • It is an equilibrium for two players. Multiway pots are computationally brutal and far less settled. A great deal of online poker, particularly in tournaments, happens three-handed to a flop where solver guidance thins out.

None of that makes solvers useless. It means a solver answers "what is safe?" rather than "what is best against this person?", and those are different questions.

Where solver study genuinely pays

The highest-return use of solver work is not memorising outputs — it is extracting the *principles* behind them, which transfer to thousands of spots you never explicitly studied.

  1. Bet sizing logic. Solvers are relentlessly clear about why range-wide small bets work on some boards and polarised large bets on others. Internalising that logic fixes more leaks than memorising any individual solution.
  2. Bluff selection. Which hands to bluff with — and specifically why blockers and backdoor equity determine the choice — is something solvers teach far better than intuition does.
  3. Defence frequencies. Understanding roughly how often you must continue against a given bet size prevents the single most common low-stakes leak: over-folding to aggression.
  4. Preflop trees. Opening ranges, 3-bet ranges, and shove-fold charts at short stacks are solved, stable, and worth knowing cold. This is the most directly memorisable and most immediately valuable output.

Preflop deserves special emphasis. It is the only part of the game where the tree is small enough that memorisation is realistic, it recurs in every single hand, and getting it wrong compounds through every later street. A player who knows their preflop ranges and nothing else about game theory will beat most low-stakes pools.

Where solvers quietly cost players money

The failure mode is predictable. A player studies solver output, sees that the equilibrium calls a river bet 38% of the time with a particular bluff-catcher, and starts making that call against a recreational opponent who has never bluffed a river in their life.

Equilibrium play is a defensive strategy. Against a player with a large, identifiable leak, it deliberately leaves money on the table. If your opponent never bluffs, the maximally profitable response is to fold every bluff-catcher — a strategy that is wildly exploitable itself, and entirely correct against that specific person.

The second failure mode is paralysis. Solver outputs are full of mixed strategies — call 62%, fold 38% — which are meaningless at an individual decision point. You cannot call 62% of the time in one hand. Players who try to replicate mixed frequencies at the table burn their clock, tilt themselves, and usually end up choosing arbitrarily anyway. The practical answer is to pick the dominant action and reserve mixing for spots where it genuinely matters.

The third is opportunity cost. Studying river subtleties in a spot that arises once every four hundred hands is a poor use of study time compared with fixing a preflop range you use every orbit, or improving your table selection so you are playing against worse opponents in the first place. Game selection remains the highest-EV skill in poker and no solver will ever tell you that. Neither will it tell you that choosing the right type of bonus for your volume can be worth more per month than a marginal river-calling improvement.

Solvers at the table: the line rooms draw

This is where the topic stops being academic. Every major poker room prohibits real-time assistance — consulting a solver, a chart, or any software output while a hand is in progress. The prohibition is near-universal across both the WPN and Chico networks and is enforced with a combination of gameplay analysis and, in some cases, client-side detection.

The rules distinguish between study and assistance, but the line varies by operator and is worth reading rather than assuming:

  • Almost always permitted: solver work away from the table, hand history review after a session, and studying preloaded solutions between games.
  • Almost always prohibited: running a solver during a hand, using real-time odds overlays, or any software that recommends an action while you are in a pot.
  • Varies by room: static preflop charts open on a second monitor. Some rooms tolerate them, some ban them outright, and the terms do not always say clearly.
  • Always prohibited: sharing a screen, seat, or decisions with another player, which most rooms treat as collusion rather than an RTA issue.

The consequences for breaching these rules are severe — confiscated balances, voided bonuses, and permanent account closure are all standard. Each operator states its policy in its terms, and the room reviews cover how aggressively each network enforces them. If you are unsure whether something you use is permitted, the cost of asking support is zero and the cost of guessing wrong is your entire balance.

A realistic study plan for a low-stakes player

For a player grinding low stakes and building a bankroll, the honest allocation of study time looks nothing like solver-heavy content suggests.

Spend the bulk of it on preflop ranges until they are automatic. Spend the next chunk reviewing your own losing hands and asking what range you were up against — the hand reading process does more for most players than any equilibrium output. Spend a smaller slice on solver principles for the flop and turn spots you actually encounter most often. And spend some of it on the parts of the game that have nothing to do with strategy at all: bankroll discipline, session length, and which games you sit in.

Solvers made poker knowledge cheap. They did not make the games unbeatable, because the average opponent has not changed nearly as much as the available tools have. The gap between what is knowable and what the typical player actually does remains enormous, and that gap is where the money is.

The bottom line

Use solvers to understand why strategies work, not to memorise what they output. Learn your preflop ranges cold, extract the sizing and bluff-selection logic, and then deviate deliberately against opponents whose mistakes you can name. Keep the tools away from the table entirely — the rules on that are unambiguous and the penalties are not worth testing.

Frequently asked questions

Are poker solvers allowed while playing online?

No. Every major poker room bans real-time assistance during a hand. Solvers are permitted for study away from the table, and breaching the rule typically means a closed account and a forfeited balance.

Do I need a solver to beat low-stakes online poker?

No. Solid preflop ranges, disciplined hand reading, and good game selection beat most low-stakes pools. Solver study adds value once those fundamentals are in place.
