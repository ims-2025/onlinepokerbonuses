Stack-to-Pot Ratio: SPR Explained for Poker

SPR tells you how committed you are before the flop is even dealt. Here is how to calculate it and use it to make cleaner decisions with big hands.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 11, 2026·9 min read

Most difficult poker decisions are difficult because they were set up badly two streets earlier. You flop top pair with a good kicker, money goes in, and by the turn you are staring at a stack-sized decision with no clear answer. The hand felt like it got complicated on its own. It did not — the complication was baked in before the flop.

Stack-to-pot ratio is the single concept that does most to clean this up. It is simple arithmetic, it takes seconds to calculate at the table, and it turns a lot of genuinely hard postflop spots into decisions you have already made. What SPR actually measures

Stack-to-pot ratio is the effective stack divided by the size of the pot, measured at the start of the flop.

Effective stack means the smaller of the two stacks still in the hand — the most either player can actually lose. If you have 200 big blinds and your opponent has 40, the effective stack is 40, because that is where the hand ends regardless of what sits in front of you.

So if the pot is 10 big blinds going to the flop and the effective stack behind is 90, your SPR is 9. If the pot is 20 and the effective stack is 40, your SPR is 2. That is the whole calculation. The value is not a strategy in itself; it is a description of how much room the hand has left to breathe.

The reason it matters is that SPR tells you how strong a hand needs to be before you can happily play for stacks. At low SPR, a moderate hand is enough. At high SPR, a moderate hand is a liability. That relationship is the entire concept.

The three SPR zones

In practice, it helps to think in bands rather than exact numbers.

  • Low SPR (roughly 0 to 3). The stacks are shallow relative to the pot. One or two bets and you are all in. Top pair, overpairs and strong draws are usually enough to commit. Implied odds barely exist because there is not enough money behind to win.
  • Medium SPR (roughly 4 to 6). The most awkward zone. Two-pair and better play cleanly, top pair becomes a one-or-two-street hand rather than a stack-off, and pot control starts to matter. Most of the genuinely hard decisions in online poker live here.
  • High SPR (roughly 7 and above). Deep relative to the pot. Stack-offs should require genuinely strong hands — sets, straights, flushes, nutted combinations. Speculative hands like suited connectors and small pairs gain enormous value because the implied odds are real.

The critical insight is that the same hand changes value completely across these bands. Top pair, top kicker is a stack-off hand at SPR 2 and a marginal one-street hand at SPR 10. Nothing about the cards changed. The money behind did.

You control SPR before the flop

This is where SPR stops being a description and becomes a tool. Your preflop raise size, and whether you raise or call at all, directly sets the SPR you will play the flop with.

A bigger preflop raise builds a bigger pot against the same stacks, which lowers SPR. A smaller raise or a flat call keeps SPR high. So when you pick your sizing, you are choosing the kind of postflop game you are about to play — and you should be choosing it deliberately based on the hand you hold.

The general principle: hands that want to get stacks in easily want low SPR. Hands that want to see cheap flops and win big pots only when they connect hard want high SPR.

  1. Big pairs and strong broadways benefit from a lower SPR, because they are usually ahead on the flop and want to reduce the number of streets an opponent can outplay or outdraw them across.
  2. Suited connectors and small pairs benefit from a higher SPR, because their value is concentrated in the rare flops where they make a monster and need deep money behind to get paid.
  3. Short-stacked opponents should pull your sizing down — raising huge into a 30 big blind stack just creates a low SPR you did not need and bloats a pot against a player who will only continue with strong holdings.
  4. Multiway pots naturally lower SPR because more players contribute preflop, which is one reason marginal hands play so much worse in family pots than heads up.

Getting this backwards is a common and expensive leak. Raising small with aces out of position against a deep stack creates a high SPR with a hand that hates high SPR — you will be playing a bloated multi-street pot with one pair and no clarity about where you stand. The commitment threshold

SPR gives you a clean rule for something players otherwise decide emotionally: at what point are you pot-committed?

The workable version is that once you have put in roughly a third or more of the effective stack, you are committed with any hand you were willing to invest that much with. A player who three-barrels and then folds to a river shove has usually either misjudged their hand strength or ignored SPR at the flop.

This is why the medium band is so uncomfortable. At SPR 5 with top pair, there is a real question about whether you want to build toward a stack-off or keep the pot controlled. Deciding on the flop — before you start putting in bets — is dramatically better than discovering the answer on the river. Ask yourself one question when the flop comes down: *if all the money goes in, am I happy?* If the answer is no, then start controlling the pot immediately rather than betting twice and folding to the third raise.

SPR in tournaments

Tournament poker is essentially a study in continuously falling SPR. Blinds rise, stacks shrink relative to the pot, and by the middle stages many pots are contested at SPR 2 or lower whether you planned it or not.

That changes hand values in a predictable direction: high-card strength and raw pair equity rise in value, and speculative implied-odds hands fall. A suited connector that is a strong profitable call 150 big blinds deep is close to unplayable at 25 big blinds, because the deep money that justified it no longer exists. The same logic underpins standard short-stack play, and it is why open-shoving becomes correct at depths where it would be absurd in a deep cash game.

Adding antes accelerates this further, since every hand starts with a larger pot relative to the stacks. A table that felt deep before antes can be genuinely shallow after them without a single stack changing size.

Putting it to work

There is no need to compute exact ratios on every hand. The habit that pays is simpler: before you act preflop, glance at the effective stack; when the flop comes, take one second to note whether you are in the shallow, awkward or deep band; then plan the hand to fit.

Do that consistently and two things happen. Your big pots start arriving with hands that want to be in big pots, and the hands that were quietly costing you money — one pair in bloated deep-stacked pots — stop appearing so often. That is a bigger win than any individual bluff you will ever pull off.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good stack-to-pot ratio?

There is no single good number. Low SPR suits big pairs and made hands, while high SPR suits speculative hands like suited connectors that need deep money to pay off.

How do I calculate SPR at the table?

Divide the smaller of the two remaining stacks by the size of the pot as the flop is dealt. A 90 big blind effective stack into a 10 big blind pot is an SPR of 9.
