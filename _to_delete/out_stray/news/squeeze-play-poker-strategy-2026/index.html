The Squeeze Play: Poker Strategy Explained

The squeeze is the most profitable bluff in online poker and most players never use it. Here is when to fire, how to size it and who to target.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 12, 2026·9 min read

A player opens from middle position. Someone flat-calls behind them. The action folds to you in the big blind, and there is now a pot sitting there with two players in it who have both shown weakness — one by opening a wide range, one by declining to raise. This is the single most exploitable preflop situation in online poker, and the overwhelming majority of players simply check or fold.

The squeeze play is what you do instead. It is a three-bet made against an opener plus at least one caller, and it wins pots at a rate that would look implausible if you had not seen the numbers. Why the squeeze works so well

The mechanics come down to the caller. When a player flat-calls a raise rather than three-betting, they are broadcasting a fairly specific range: hands too good to fold, not good enough to raise. Medium pairs, suited connectors, suited broadways, sometimes ace-jack. Notably absent from that range are the hands that want to play a big preflop pot — aces, kings and ace-king would nearly always have raised.

So when you three-bet, the caller is caught in a genuine vice. They have already committed money, they are facing a raise, and they hold a hand that plays badly against aggression. They also have the original raiser still to act behind them, which means they cannot simply call with a speculative holding and hope to see a cheap flop. That pressure is where the name comes from.

Meanwhile the original raiser faces a three-bet knowing that a player behind them has already expressed interest. Their marginal opens — suited gappers, weak aces, small pairs from late position — become straightforward folds. Two players, both under pressure, both with capped ranges. The pot is frequently uncontested.

The extra dead money

There is a second reason the squeeze outperforms an ordinary three-bet: the pot is bigger before you even act.

A standard three-bet against a single opener is fighting for the open plus the blinds. A squeeze is fighting for the open, the call, and the blinds. That additional call is dead money that materially improves the price your bluff needs to work at.

Run the arithmetic roughly. If you squeeze to 12 big blinds into a pot containing an open of 3, a call of 3, and blinds of 1.5, you are risking 12 to win 7.5 immediately. That needs to succeed around 62% of the time to break even as a pure bluff — and against two capped ranges, well-chosen squeezes come in comfortably above that. Every time you also happen to hold a real hand, the equity is pure profit on top.

Which hands to squeeze with

The instinct is to squeeze with the hands that feel strong, but that gets the construction backwards. Your squeezing range should be deliberately polarised: very strong hands and specific bluff candidates, with the middling stuff mostly left out.

  1. Premium value. Aces, kings, queens, ace-king. These squeeze for value and want the money in. Balancing them with bluffs is what stops observant opponents from folding everything to your three-bets.
  2. Suited aces. A5s through A2s and the suited broadway aces are the classic squeeze bluffs. They hold blocker value — you remove some of the ace-x combinations that would continue — and they flop enough equity to keep barrelling.
  3. Suited broadways. KQs, KJs, QJs work well because they dominate parts of the caller's flatting range and make strong top pairs when called.
  4. Hands to leave out. Small and medium pairs, offsuit broadways and most suited connectors are generally better as calls or folds. They have poor blocker properties and they play awkwardly in a bloated three-bet pot out of position.

Notice that the bluff selection is not random. Blockers do real work here. Holding an ace makes it meaningfully less likely that either opponent holds a premium ace, which is precisely the holding that would put your squeeze in trouble.

Sizing it properly

A squeeze needs to be bigger than a standard three-bet. This is the most common execution error — players use their normal three-bet size and wonder why the caller keeps coming along.

The reason is simple: you are applying pressure to two players, and the caller is already getting a discount because their money is in. A workable baseline is roughly four times the original open when you are out of position, and about three and a half times when you are in position, adding a big blind or so for each additional caller in the pot.

So against a 3 big blind open with one caller from the big blind, something around 12 to 13 big blinds. Against the same open with two callers, closer to 15. Going smaller invites exactly the multiway flop you were trying to avoid, and multiway pots are where squeeze bluffs go to die.

Picking the right targets

Squeeze frequency should not be uniform. The spot is only reliably profitable against certain opponent profiles, and reading the table before you fire is most of the skill.

  • Late-position openers. A cutoff or button open is far wider than an under-the-gun open, so there is much more of their range that must fold. Squeeze these relentlessly; be careful against early-position raisers.
  • Passive, sticky callers. Ironically the loose-passive flat-caller is a poor target for a bluff squeeze — they call too much. Value-squeeze them instead and dial the bluffs down.
  • Tight, straightforward callers. These are the ideal target. Their flatting range is genuinely capped and they fold it honestly to pressure.
  • Stack depth. Squeezing works best around 80 to 150 big blinds. Very short and the three-bet commits you; very deep and the caller gets implied odds to continue with speculative hands.

Table dynamics feed directly into this. Softer, more passive player pools produce more of the raise-and-flat sequences the squeeze feeds on, which is one practical reason to care about game quality when choosing where to play — our room reviews cover traffic and table softness alongside the offers.

Position also matters more than most players account for. A squeeze from the button or cutoff carries the additional threat of postflop position, which increases fold equity. Squeezing from the blinds still works, but you should tighten the bluff selection because you will be playing the rest of the hand out of position.

Playing the flop after you get called

Roughly a third of the time somebody calls, and a plan for what comes next is what separates a profitable squeeze from a leak.

The good news is that you hold a strong range advantage on most boards. Your polarised squeezing range contains far more premium hands than the caller's capped one, which means high-card and ace-high flops belong to you. A continuation bet of around a third to a half of the pot on those textures picks up an enormous number of pots.

Low, connected boards are the ones to slow down on. A 7-6-5 with two hearts hits a flatting range of suited connectors and middle pairs far harder than it hits yours. Checking there and giving up cheaply costs you a fraction of what stubbornly firing three barrels into the caller's best texture will.

The same stack-to-pot ratio logic that governs any three-bet pot applies: at these depths the SPR after a squeeze is typically low, so top pair is often a stack-off hand rather than a marginal one.

Getting the frequency right

The failure modes sit at both extremes. Never squeezing means leaving a genuinely free source of edge on the table every session. Squeezing every time you see a raise-and-call means running into aces and getting stacked repeatedly by opponents who have noticed.

The practical target for most online cash games is squeezing something in the region of eight to twelve percent of the time the spot arises, weighted heavily toward late-position openers and tight callers. Start conservatively, watch how each opponent responds, and widen against players who fold. Once a table adjusts and starts four-betting you, tighten back to value and let them pay you off.

The squeeze is not an advanced trick reserved for high stakes. It is a fundamental spot that appears dozens of times in an ordinary session and goes unclaimed at almost every table below the highest limits. Learn the three components — target selection, polarised range, larger sizing — and the pots start arriving without a showdown. Frequently asked questions

What is a squeeze play in poker?

A squeeze is a three-bet made after one player has raised and at least one other has called. The extra dead money and the caller's capped range make it unusually profitable.

How big should a squeeze bet be?

Larger than a standard three-bet — roughly four times the open out of position and three and a half times in position, adding about a big blind for each extra caller.
