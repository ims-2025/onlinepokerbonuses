Suited Connectors: When to Play and When to Fold

Suited connectors look fun and lose money in the wrong spots. Here is how position, stack depth and rake decide whether they are profitable online.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 17, 2026·9 min read

Few hands cost recreational players more money than suited connectors. Not because the hands are bad — they are genuinely excellent in the right conditions — but because the conditions are narrower than most people playing them realise. Seven-six of hearts is a monster in a deep-stacked multiway pot in position. The same hand is a slow leak from under the gun with forty big blinds behind, and the difference between those two spots is worth more to your win rate than almost any postflop adjustment you could make.

This piece is about telling the two apart. Not a chart to memorise — the useful thing is the reasoning, because the reasoning transfers to every stake and format you play, whether you are grinding volume to clear a welcome offer or just trying to stop bleeding chips in spots that feel exciting.

What you are actually buying with a suited connector

A suited connector is a lottery ticket with a small, specific payout structure. It rarely wins a small pot and occasionally wins an enormous one. Everything about playing it well follows from that shape.

Start with the raw numbers, because they are less flattering than the reputation. A suited hand flops a flush roughly once in a hundred. It flops a four-card flush draw about eleven percent of the time. Connected cards flop an open-ended straight draw somewhere around ten percent of the time, and a made straight far less than that. Combine everything and you flop something genuinely strong — a made hand you are happy to stack off with, or a big combo draw — in the neighbourhood of one hand in six.

Which means five times out of six you are folding, or making a small continuation play and giving up. The hand is not profitable because it hits often. It is profitable because of what happens the one time in six that it does, and only if the pot is big enough on those occasions to pay for the other five.

  • Implied odds are the entire case. You need opponents who will pay you off when you make your hand.
  • Reverse implied odds are the entire risk. Second-best flushes and idiot-end straights are how these hands lose stacks, not how they win them.
  • Position multiplies both. Acting last lets you realise equity cheaply and control the pot size when you miss.
  • Stack depth sets the ceiling. You cannot win a big pot that the stacks do not allow to exist.

The stack depth test

Before anything else, look at the effective stack. This single number does more to determine whether a suited connector is playable than the specific cards do.

At 100 big blinds or deeper, suited connectors do their job. There is room to call a raise, hit a draw, and win a pot several times the size of your preflop investment. At 40 big blinds, that room is gone. You call three big blinds, flop a flush draw, and the maximum you can win is a stack that barely rewards the risk — while the hands that beat you, the overpairs and top pairs, get to realise their equity in full.

The practical rule most solid players use is that speculative hands scale with depth. Deep games make them better; short stacks make them worse. This is why suited connectors are far more valuable in a deep cash game than in the late stages of a tournament, where stacks compress and the hands that matter are the ones that can win a pot without improving. There is a related point about tournaments worth stating plainly. Early levels with 150 big blind stacks are the right conditions for suited connectors. Levels where antes are on and stacks have shrunk to 25 blinds are not, however tempting the hand looks when it arrives.

Position is not a tiebreaker, it is the decision

The gap between playing a suited connector in position and out of position is larger than the gap between a good and a bad suited connector. Nine-eight suited on the button outperforms jack-ten suited from under the gun by a comfortable margin, and the reason is equity realisation.

Out of position, you check into the aggressor, you face bets before you have information, and you are frequently forced to fold hands with real equity because calling twice with a gutshot out of position is a losing proposition. You realise far less of your hand's theoretical equity than the raw number suggests.

In position, everything reverses. You see what your opponent does before acting. You can take a free card with a draw. You can bet when checked to twice and win the pot without a hand. You can control the final pot size so that your missed draws cost you little and your made hands get maximum value. The same 35 percent equity is simply worth more when you act last.

The practical consequence: open suited connectors from late position, call with them in position, and be considerably more selective from early seats and the blinds. Cold-calling a raise from the small blind with six-five suited is one of the most reliably unprofitable actions in online poker, and it happens constantly.

The rake factor nobody mentions

Here is the consideration that separates online play from the theory you read about, and it matters especially at the stakes where most bonus clearing happens. Rake hits small pots hardest, and suited connectors win small pots most often when they win at all before the big one lands.

At micro and low stakes, the rake taken from an average pot represents a meaningful percentage of the pot. Marginal speculative hands that would be break-even or slightly profitable in a rake-free game become clearly losing once the house takes its cut. This is not a small effect — it is the single biggest reason that winning strategies at $0.05/$0.10 look tighter than the theoretically optimal ranges suggest.

The implication is straightforward: play fewer suited connectors at low stakes than a solver would, especially from out of position, and more of them as you move up and the rake percentage falls. How to play them after the flop

Assume you have picked a good spot — late position, deep stacks, a raiser who plays too straightforwardly. What now?

  1. Categorise the flop immediately. Did you make a hand, a strong draw, a weak draw, or nothing? Weak draws are the money pit. A gutshot with no backdoor flush is a fold to any real aggression, not a call.
  2. Respect the quality of your draw. A flush draw to the ace behaves very differently from one to the seven. The nut draw can be played aggressively; the low one frequently cannot.
  3. Be honest about straight ends. Flopping the bottom end of a straight on a coordinated board is a trap, not a jackpot. These hands lose stacks with monotonous regularity.
  4. Use your position to take free cards. Checking behind with a draw on a flop where your opponent will barrel two streets is often better than calling twice.
  5. Balance your semi-bluffs. Strong draws are your best raising hands precisely because you have equity when called. This is where the hand class earns most of its value.

Notice how much of that list is about folding cheaply. Playing suited connectors well is mostly about losing the minimum in the five spots out of six where they miss, not about extracting maximum value in the sixth. Players who focus only on the payoff end up funding everyone else's.

A simple filter before you call

When a suited connector arrives and the temptation kicks in, ask three questions. Are the effective stacks at least 80 to 100 big blinds? Will I be in position for the rest of the hand? Is there at least one opponent in the pot who will pay off a big hand? Two yeses out of three is marginal. Three is a clear call. One is a fold, however pretty the cards look.

That filter will cut your suited connector volume substantially, and your win rate will go up rather than down. The hands you discard were the ones costing you money; the ones you keep are the ones the hand class was designed for.

Frequently asked questions

Are suited connectors profitable in online poker?

They can be, but only in position with deep stacks against opponents who pay off big hands. Out of position or short-stacked, they usually lose money.

Should I play suited connectors at micro stakes?

Play them selectively. Rake takes a larger share of small pots, which pushes marginal speculative hands from slightly profitable into losing territory.
