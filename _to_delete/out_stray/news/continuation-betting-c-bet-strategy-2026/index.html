Continuation Betting: How to C-Bet Profitably

The c-bet is the most used and most misused move in online poker. Learn which flops to fire on, which to check, and how sizing changes everything.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 2, 2026·8 min read

The continuation bet is the first decision most players make after the flop and the one they think least about. You raised preflop, you got called, the flop comes, and you bet — because that is what you do. It is so automatic that many players could not articulate why they are doing it beyond a vague sense that the aggressor is supposed to keep the pressure on.

That reflex is why c-betting is simultaneously the most profitable and the most costly habit in low-stakes online poker. Fired at the right frequency on the right boards, it prints money. Fired at every flop regardless of texture, it hands money to anyone paying attention. The gap between those two outcomes is worth more to your win rate than almost any other single adjustment, and considerably more than the marginal edge you get from grinding out the extra value covered in our bonus types guide — though ideally you take both.

What a c-bet is actually doing

A continuation bet works for two distinct reasons, and it is essential to know which one you are relying on in any given hand.

Fold equity. You have the preflop initiative, which means your range contains the strong hands your opponent's range mostly does not — big pairs, strong aces, premium broadways. When the flop misses your opponent, which it does most of the time, they have to give up. A bet collects the pot immediately.

Value. When you do connect, betting builds a pot against worse hands that will call. This is the straightforward half, and it is the half players get wrong less often.

The mistake is treating those as the same thing. A bet that relies on fold equity needs your opponent to be capable of folding, and needs the board to plausibly favour your range. A bet for value needs a worse hand that will call. When neither condition holds, you are burning chips, and the flop texture is what tells you which condition you are in.

Board texture: the whole ballgame

The single biggest improvement most players can make is to stop asking "did I hit?" and start asking "whose range does this flop favour?" Those are different questions and the second one is the useful one.

Boards that favour the preflop raiser are dry, high-card, disconnected textures — an ace or king high flop with two low, unpaired, unsuited cards. Your raising range is packed with hands that connect here. Your opponent's calling range is not. These are the flops where a c-bet works even with nothing at all, because the story is credible and your opponent's range is full of hands that cannot continue.

Boards that favour the caller are low, connected, coordinated textures — middling cards in sequence, especially suited ones. A flop like nine-eight-seven with two of a suit hits a calling range of suited connectors and small pairs far harder than it hits a range built around big cards. Firing here with air is not aggression, it is a donation, and against a competent opponent it is a donation that repeats on the turn.

Paired boards are their own category and are frequently misread. They favour whoever has more of the trips combinations, but more importantly they reduce the number of hands that can comfortably call, which makes small bets effective across a wide range.

Sizing tells the story

Betting the same fraction of the pot on every flop is the second most common leak after betting too often. Sizing should follow from texture and from what you are trying to accomplish.

  • Small bets on dry boards. When your opponent's range is weak and disconnected, you do not need a big bet to fold out hands that have nothing. A third of the pot achieves the same fold equity at a third of the risk, and lets you bet a wider range.
  • Larger bets on wet boards. When you have a strong hand on a coordinated texture, charge the draws. A small bet gives correct odds to hands that will beat you when they hit.
  • Consistent sizing within a texture. Betting big only with strong hands and small only with air is a pattern observant opponents will read within a session.
  • Overbets are situational, not decorative. They work when your range is dramatically stronger than your opponent's, which is rarer than the players who love them believe.

The underlying principle is that your sizing should be a function of the board, not of your hand. If an opponent can infer your holding from how much you bet, the sizing itself is losing you money regardless of how sound the individual decision was.

The checking range you do not have

Strong players check a meaningful portion of their flops, and it is not weakness — it is what makes the bets work. If you c-bet one hundred per cent of the time, your betting range is simply your entire range, which means it contains no information and your opponent can attack it freely. Checking some hands gives your bets meaning.

What belongs in a checking range:

  1. Hands with showdown value that cannot stand a raise. Middle pair on a coordinated board is often better off checking twice and getting to showdown than betting and folding to pressure.
  2. Air on boards that smash the caller's range. Give up. There is no shame in it, and it saves the money that funds the flops where you do have an edge.
  3. Occasional strong hands. A slowplay now and again protects the checking range from being attacked automatically, though this can be overdone.
  4. Marginal draws in bad spots. Not every draw needs to be a semi-bluff, particularly out of position against an opponent who calls widely.

That first category deserves emphasis. Betting middle pair for "protection" and then folding to a raise is one of the most reliably losing sequences in low-stakes poker. The hand had value; the bet turned it into a bluff-catcher that got bluff-raised off its equity.

Adjusting to the player, not the theory

All of the above describes sound baseline play. Actual profit comes from deviating against specific opponents, and online poker gives you the sample size to do it.

Against a player who folds too much, c-bet wider than theory suggests and keep firing on the turn. Against a station who calls everything, cut the bluffs entirely and bet relentlessly for value with anything decent. Against an aggressive opponent who raises flops frequently, check strong hands more often and let them bluff into you. These adjustments are worth far more than any refinement of a balanced default.

This is also why table selection matters as much as technique. A perfectly executed c-betting strategy against tough regulars earns less than a merely competent one in a soft game — a point that applies equally to where you choose to play, which is why the traffic and game-quality notes in our Americas Cardroom review and Black Chip Poker review are worth reading before you pick a room.

Putting it together

If you take one thing away, make it this: before you reach for the bet button on the flop, ask whose range the board favours. That single question, asked consistently, will fix more c-betting mistakes than any amount of studying frequencies.

Bet the flops that hit your range, check the ones that hit theirs, size according to texture rather than hand strength, and adjust hard against opponents who deviate from balance. Do that with reasonable discipline and your postflop win rate improves without learning a single new concept.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I continuation bet?

There is no single number. Bet frequently on dry, high-card boards that favour your preflop range, and much less often on low, connected boards that favour the caller.

What is the most common c-betting mistake?

Betting every flop automatically. It makes your betting range transparent and donates money on coordinated boards that connect with a calling range.
