Blind Stealing From the Button and Cutoff in Poker

Stealing the blinds from late position is one of poker's easiest edges. Learn ranges, sizing and adjustments for the button and cutoff at online tables.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published September 29, 2026·8 min read

Every hand at an online poker table starts with two players who have already paid to be there. The small blind and big blind are dead money sitting in the middle, and the players acting last before them have the first chance to take it. Raising to win those blinds without a fight is called blind stealing, and it is one of the simplest, most reliable sources of profit in the game.

This guide covers why stealing works, how wide to open from the cutoff and button, what sizes to use, and how to adjust when the blinds fight back. It is also a useful skill for bonus hunters: a steady stream of uncontested pots keeps your win rate healthy while you build volume on an offer from our best poker bonuses list.

Why stealing the blinds is profitable

The maths behind a steal is straightforward. If you raise to 2.5 big blinds and everyone folds, you win 1.5 big blinds. That is a gain every time the blinds give up, and blinds fold a large share of the time because they are out of position and holding random cards.

Suppose you open to 2.5 big blinds and the blinds fold together about 55 percent of the time. You risk 2.5 to win 1.5, so you need them to fold only around 62 percent of the time to make the raise profitable with any two cards on that alone. Because you also have equity, position and initiative when you are called, hands you would never play from early position become profitable opens on the button.

Position drives everything. On the button you act last on every postflop street, which means you see what your opponent does before you decide. That informational edge is worth real money over thousands of hands, and it is why late-position ranges are so much wider than early-position ranges.

Ranges by seat: cutoff and button

Exact ranges depend on the game, the rake and your opponents, but a sensible starting framework for six-max cash games at 100 big blinds looks like this:

  • Cutoff: roughly 25 to 30 percent of hands. That includes all pairs, most suited aces, suited broadway cards, suited connectors down to around 65s, and offsuit broadway hands.
  • Button: roughly 40 to 50 percent of hands when the blinds are passive. Add more suited gappers, weaker suited kings and queens, and offsuit connectors such as T9o and 98o.
  • Small blind vs big blind only: the small blind can open wider still, but out-of-position play makes this a different problem, usually best solved with raise-or-fold discipline and a larger size.

Treat those percentages as guidelines rather than commandments. Solver-derived charts are a good reference, and our overview of poker solvers and training tools explains how to study them without memorising blindly. What matters most is that your opening range is wider on the button than the cutoff, and wider in the cutoff than in the hijack.

Sizing your steal raise

Online, the standard open size is 2 to 2.5 big blinds from the button and 2.5 big blinds from the cutoff, with a slightly larger size when the blinds are aggressive. Smaller sizes risk less to win the same blinds, which improves your fold-equity maths, but they also give the big blind better odds to defend.

Adjust sizes based on who is behind you:

  1. Against tight blinds who fold too often: open a little smaller, around 2 big blinds, and widen your range.
  2. Against blinds who defend and three-bet often: use the standard 2.5 big blinds and tighten the bottom of your range slightly.
  3. With antes or straddles in play: increase your range because there is more dead money to win.
  4. At deeper stacks: favour hands with strong postflop potential such as suited connectors and suited aces.

Choosing hands: playability over raw strength

Most of the profit from stealing comes from the weakest hands in your range, so choose them carefully. Prefer hands that make strong draws, keep blockers to strong holdings, or flop well enough to continue. Suited cards, connected cards and hands with an ace are all better than disconnected offsuit junk.

Blockers matter more than many players realise. An ace in your hand makes it less likely that the big blind holds a premium hand to call or three-bet with. Our article on blockers and card removal explains why hands like A5s and K9s outperform hands such as J4o even when equity is similar.

Dealing with three-bets from the blinds

The main cost of stealing is that thoughtful opponents will three-bet you. If a player is doing so too often, your response is not to stop opening but to adjust how you defend. Some practical guidelines:

  • Fold the weakest hands in your opening range facing a three-bet, particularly offsuit hands and small suited gappers.
  • Call with hands that play well in position, such as suited connectors, medium pairs and suited broadway hands.
  • Four-bet bluff selectively with suited aces, which block your opponent's strongest holdings and keep your range balanced.
  • Track the stats: if a blind three-bets more than roughly 12 percent, you can call and four-bet a little wider; if under 5 percent, respect their range and fold more.

Our strategy guide on 3-bet pots covers the postflop side of these battles in more detail, while the squeeze play article shows how stealing from later positions changes when someone has already opened.

Postflop: making the steal pay off

Winning the blinds preflop is only part of the job. When the big blind calls, you now have position, the initiative and a range advantage on many boards. Continuation bet small on dry, high-card flops where you hold the range advantage, and slow down on low, connected boards that favour the defender.

Do not autopilot every bet. Against a big blind who calls often but folds to turn pressure, plan to barrel with draws and give up with weak hands that have no equity. Against a sticky player, value bet thinner and bluff less. Our guide to continuation betting provides board-by-board guidance.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Stealing into the wrong opponents. A short-stacked, aggressive big blind is a poor target; a nit who folds constantly is ideal.
  • Opening too wide from the cutoff because you copy button ranges. Two seats behind, the button is still to act.
  • Ignoring the rake. At higher rake percentages, marginal opens lose value, so tighten the bottom of your range at expensive tables.
  • Giving up too easily postflop. A steal that folds to every continuation bet still profits, but you leave money behind if you never follow through.

Turn stealing into a habit

Blind stealing rewards patience and observation rather than fancy plays. Open a wider range from the button and cutoff, size sensibly, fold hands that do not belong when you are three-bet, and follow through postflop when the situation calls for it. Over thousands of hands, those small edges add up to a meaningful win rate. Frequently asked questions

How wide should I open on the button?

Around 40 to 50 percent of hands is a common starting point in six-max at 100 big blinds. Tighten against aggressive blinds and widen against passive ones.

What is the best size for a steal raise?

Two to 2.5 big blinds is standard online. Go smaller against players who fold too much and stick to 2.5 when the blinds defend often.
