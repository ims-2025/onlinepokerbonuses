Tournament Tickets vs Cash Bonuses: Which Pays More?

Poker rooms increasingly pay welcome value in tournament tickets instead of cash. Here is how to price a ticket and work out which offer suits you.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published August 24, 2026·10 min read

Somewhere in the last few years the standard US-facing poker welcome package quietly stopped being a single number. Where a room once advertised a straightforward percentage match and left it there, the modern offer is usually a bundle: a matched deposit bonus that releases against rake, plus a stack of tournament tickets, plus entry into a new-depositor freeroll series. The bundle looks more generous. Whether it *is* more generous depends entirely on how you value the tickets, and most players never do that arithmetic at all.

Tickets are not cash. They cannot be withdrawn, they usually expire, and they are only worth anything if you actually register for the event they unlock at a time you were willing to play. Rooms know this, which is why tickets are such an attractive currency for them to pay in — the advertised value is high and the redemption rate is low. That does not make ticket-heavy packages a bad deal, but it does mean you need a way to price them before comparing offers on our best poker bonuses page.

What a tournament ticket is actually worth

The face value of a ticket is the buy-in of the tournament it enters. A ticket for a $22 event is advertised as being worth $22. Its expected value to you is a different and always smaller number, and it is a product of four things.

  1. Redemption probability. Will you genuinely play that tournament, at that start time, before the ticket expires? If a $22 ticket unlocks a Sunday 6pm event and you never play Sundays, its value is zero. Be honest here — this is where most of the theoretical value evaporates.
  2. Field quality and your edge. A ticket into a soft new-depositor freeroll is worth more per dollar of face value than a ticket into a high-buy-in event stacked with regulars. Your realistic return in a field of experienced players may be well under the buy-in.
  3. Opportunity cost. If you would have registered for that tournament anyway with your own money, the ticket is worth close to full face value. If it pulls you into a game you would otherwise have skipped, it is worth much less — you are spending time, not just using a voucher.
  4. Expiry and drip. Many packages release tickets weekly rather than all at once, so a package of eight tickets might arrive over two months. Miss a week and that week's ticket is simply gone.

A workable rule of thumb: for a player who plays tournaments regularly at roughly the buy-in level of the tickets, real value lands somewhere near half to two-thirds of face value. For a cash-game player who rarely registers for anything, it is closer to zero. That is a big enough spread that it flips the ranking between offers.

Why rooms prefer paying in tickets

There is nothing sinister about ticket-based packages, but it helps to understand the economics from the other side of the table. Paying a welcome bonus in cash costs the room the full amount the moment it releases. Paying in tickets costs the room far less than face value, for three reasons.

  • Breakage. A meaningful share of tickets are never used. Every unused ticket costs the room nothing at all.
  • Rake recapture. A player who enters a tournament on a ticket often re-enters, plays satellites, or moves to the cash tables afterwards. The ticket is a customer-acquisition cost that generates rake.
  • Prize-pool structure. In many ticket-funded events the room is adding overlay only if the guarantee is not met, so the marginal cost of an extra entrant can be close to nothing.

The upshot for you is simple: ticket value is advertised at retail and delivered at wholesale. Any comparison that takes ticket face value at par will systematically overrate ticket-heavy packages against straight cash matches.

Comparing a bundle against a plain match

Put two realistic offers side by side. Offer A is a large percentage match with a four-figure ceiling, released incrementally as you generate rake, with no tickets. Offer B is a smaller match plus a package of tournament tickets and freeroll entries. Which pays more?

Work it in three steps. First, estimate the cash you will actually release from each match — not the ceiling, but the amount your realistic volume will unlock before the clearing window closes. This is the calculation we walk through in detail in our guide to the different poker bonus types, and for most recreational players it comes out at a small fraction of the headline. Second, discount the ticket component using the redemption logic above. Third, compare the two adjusted totals.

Run honestly, this exercise produces one of two clear answers. A tournament player with regular weekly volume at the relevant buy-in level will often find Offer B wins, because their tickets convert at close to full value and they were going to register anyway. A cash-game grinder or an infrequent player will usually find Offer A wins comfortably, because tickets they will not use are worth nothing and a faster cash release rate is worth a lot.

The freeroll component deserves separate treatment

New-depositor freeroll series are the one part of these packages that is almost universally underrated, and for a specific structural reason: the fields are restricted to recent depositors, which means they are unusually soft, and the entry cost is genuinely zero rather than nominally zero.

The catch is scheduling. Restricted freerolls typically run on a fixed weekly slot for a limited number of weeks after your deposit. If those slots do not fit your life, the series is worth nothing to you; if they do, it may be the single highest-value component of the whole package. Before you deposit anywhere, check the freeroll calendar and ask whether you will realistically be at a computer at those times. Timing your deposit so the series starts on a week you are free is a small piece of planning that costs nothing and can be worth more than the match bonus.

Practical rules for ticket-heavy packages

A few habits separate players who extract real value from ticket bundles from those who let them lapse.

  • Diary the expiry dates the day they land. Tickets expire quietly. Nothing reminds you.
  • Never chase a ticket into a game you should not be in. Registering for a tournament three levels above your normal buy-in because 'it's free' is how a $109 ticket costs you a re-entry and a tilted cash session.
  • Use satellites deliberately. Some tickets can be spent on satellites into much larger events. For a player with a genuine edge in short-field satellites this multiplies the value; for everyone else it converts a near-certain small ticket into a lottery ticket.
  • Check whether unused tickets convert. A minority of promotions let expiring tickets be exchanged for smaller-value entries or loyalty points. It is worth a message to support before the deadline.
  • Do not let tickets dictate your room choice if you are a cash player. Software, traffic at your stakes and cashout speed will matter far more over a year.

Room-by-room differences worth knowing

Ticket-heavy packages cluster around rooms with strong tournament schedules, because that is where the marginal entrant is cheapest to serve. Rooms on the Winning Poker Network tend to lean on their flagship series to drive ticket promotions, while Chico Network rooms tend to distribute value across smaller weekly events and leaderboards. Neither approach is inherently better — they suit different players.

If you are primarily a tournament player, look at the schedule before the bonus: a package of tickets into events that run when you play is worth more than a nominally larger package into events you will miss. Our reviews of BetOnline Poker and TigerGaming set out the current schedules and promotional mix at two of the Chico rooms, and the same discipline applies to whichever room you end up choosing.

One more thing worth checking regardless of room: whether the tickets are tied to the same clearing window as the cash bonus. When both run on the same clock, a slow month can cost you both components at once, and a player who deposits just before a busy period at work has effectively bought a smaller package than the one on the banner.

The bottom line

Tickets are real value, but they are conditional value, and the condition is you. Price them at what you will actually redeem rather than what the banner says, discount for expiry and for fields you have no edge in, and then compare adjusted totals rather than headline totals. Do that and the choice between a plain cash match and a loaded bundle stops being a matter of which graphic looks more impressive.

Frequently asked questions

Are tournament tickets worth as much as the cash value they advertise?

Rarely. A ticket is only worth its face value if you would have registered for that event anyway; for most players, realistic value lands somewhere between zero and about two-thirds of face value once expiry and field quality are factored in.

Do unused poker tournament tickets expire?

Almost always, and often within weeks of being credited. Note the expiry date as soon as a ticket lands and check whether your room allows expiring tickets to be exchanged for smaller entries or loyalty points.
