Poker Overlays Explained: Guarantees Worth Playing

An overlay is the closest thing online poker has to free money. Here is what causes one, how to spot it early and why chasing them can still cost you.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published August 27, 2026·10 min read

When a poker room advertises a tournament with a guaranteed prize pool, it is making a promise that it will pay out at least that amount regardless of how many people enter. Most of the time the promise costs the room nothing, because enough players register that the buy-ins cover the guarantee comfortably. Occasionally they do not — and when the entry fees fall short, the room tops up the difference out of its own pocket. That top-up is an overlay, and it is the closest thing in online poker to money handed to the players for nothing.

Overlays get talked about with a certain reverence in poker forums, and the enthusiasm is broadly justified: extra prize money added to a field is a genuine, quantifiable increase in every entrant's expected value. But the way most players act on that information is careless, and the careless version can cost more than the overlay adds. This piece covers what actually creates an overlay, how to find one before registration closes, and the specific traps that turn a positive-value opportunity into a losing habit.

Where an overlay comes from

The arithmetic is simple. A tournament with a guarantee needs a certain number of entries at a given buy-in to fund that guarantee out of player money. If a room guarantees a prize pool and the buy-ins collected fall below it, the shortfall is added by the operator. Divide that shortfall by the number of entrants and you have the per-player overlay — a straight addition to the value of every seat in the field.

Rooms guarantee prize pools for the same reason supermarkets run loss leaders: the guarantee is a marketing promise that makes the tournament worth registering for. A schedule full of guarantees signals a healthy, liquid site. The operator accepts the risk of occasional top-ups as the cost of that signal, and prices most guarantees conservatively enough that the risk rarely materialises.

The conditions that actually produce overlays

Overlays are not random. They cluster around predictable circumstances, and knowing the circumstances is more useful than refreshing lobbies hoping to get lucky.

  • Newly launched tournaments. A room adding an event to its schedule has to guess at demand. Guess high and the first few weeks produce overlays until the guarantee is recalibrated or the tournament finds its audience.
  • Awkward start times. Events scheduled outside the peak evening window for the room's main player base draw thinner fields. A guarantee written for a peak-time audience and run at an off-peak hour is a recurring overlay candidate.
  • Big series with crowded schedules. During a large series, dozens of events run in parallel. Attention concentrates on the flagship events and the smaller side tournaments — particularly the ones with unusual formats or high buy-ins — regularly come up short.
  • Competing events elsewhere. A major televised final, a large live festival, or a rival network running its own series can pull volume away on a specific night.
  • Format resistance. Guarantees on formats with narrower appeal — some mixed games, deep-stacked marathons, unusual bounty structures — miss more often than a standard no-limit hold'em event at the same buy-in.

None of these guarantee an overlay. They simply describe the conditions under which the room's demand forecast is most likely to be wrong, which is the only thing that produces one.

How to spot an overlay before it is too late

The practical skill is reading a lobby. Most poker clients display the current prize pool alongside the guarantee, and the gap between the two is the overlay so far. The complication is late registration: a tournament showing a large shortfall at the start can fill that gap entirely during the late-reg window, so the number you see at the first hand is not final.

That makes the timing decision genuinely tricky. Register early and you commit before knowing whether the overlay will survive. Wait until late registration is nearly closed and you know the real number — but you enter with a shorter stack relative to the blinds, which costs you equity in a different way. The short version is that the stack cost of waiting is real and needs to be weighed against the information gained, and neither side of that trade is negligible.

A more reliable approach than lobby-watching is record-keeping. Tournaments that overlay tend to overlay repeatedly, because the conditions producing them — the start time, the format, the position in the schedule — persist week to week. Note which events came up short and check the same ones next week. That is a far better use of attention than scanning every lobby on the site.

Why chasing overlays goes wrong

Here is the part the enthusiasm usually skips. An overlay increases the value of a seat, but it does not change any of the other reasons a tournament might be a bad spot for you specifically, and three of those reasons routinely outweigh it.

Buy-in relative to bankroll. An overlay on a tournament well above your normal stake is not an invitation to play it. Tournament variance is brutal at the best of times, and a modest edge added to a buy-in you cannot properly afford is a straightforwardly bad trade. The same logic governs bonus clearing, where moving up to release a bonus faster is the classic version of the mistake — our bonus types guide sets out why the release schedule should never dictate your stake.

Field composition. The tournaments that most often overlay are frequently the ones recreational players avoid — unusual formats, high buy-ins, awkward times. A thin field at an inconvenient hour is disproportionately made up of regulars who are also there for the overlay. Extra prize money split among tougher opponents may leave you worse off than a full field of casual players with no overlay at all.

Time cost. Registering for events you would not otherwise play, at hours you would not otherwise play them, in order to collect a small per-seat edge, is a reliable route to fatigue. Playing tired is expensive in a way that dwarfs most overlays, a few bad decisions at the end of a long night erase a great many small edges. Overlays, bonuses and rakeback pull in the same direction

The useful way to think about an overlay is as one component in the total value of a session rather than a headline in itself. Tournament buy-ins generate rake, that rake feeds your bonus clearing and your ongoing rakeback, and an overlay sits on top of both. A player with an active welcome bonus releasing against volume is collecting three things from the same entry fee: the tournament equity, the bonus release, and the loyalty accrual.

That stacking is why the players who extract the most from overlays are rarely the ones hunting them. They are the ones already playing a consistent schedule at a room whose promotions suit them, who happen to be registered when a guarantee falls short. A sensible overlay policy

Play the events you were going to play anyway, at stakes your bankroll supports, at times you can play well. Keep a simple record of which tournaments come up short and treat that list as a nudge toward one schedule over another when the choice is otherwise even. Take an overlay when it appears in a spot you already wanted to be in, and let it pass when it does not.

That is a deeply unglamorous policy and it captures nearly all of the available value, because the value was always small per seat and only ever added up through volume. The dramatic version — sitting up until three in the morning to enter a high buy-in event because the lobby shows a shortfall — is how a genuine edge gets converted into a loss. Frequently asked questions

What is an overlay in online poker?

An overlay happens when the entry fees collected for a guaranteed tournament fall short of the guarantee, so the poker room adds the difference itself. That top-up is extra prize money shared among the entrants.

Should I always play a tournament with an overlay?

No. An overlay adds value, but it does not fix a buy-in that is too large for your bankroll, a field full of regulars or a start time that has you playing tired. Take overlays in events you would sensibly play anyway.
