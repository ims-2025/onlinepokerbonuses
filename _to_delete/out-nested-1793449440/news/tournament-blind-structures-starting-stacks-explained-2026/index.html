Tournament Blind Structures and Starting Stacks Explained

Starting stacks, blind levels and level length decide how much skill a poker tournament rewards. Learn how to judge structure before you register.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 30, 2026·8 min read

Two tournaments with the same buy-in and the same guarantee can be completely different products. One gives you a deep stack, slow blinds and hours to outplay your opponents. The other hands you a short stack, fast blinds and a game that is mostly shoving within an hour. The difference is the blind structure, and it is the most overlooked line in the tournament lobby. Learning to read it will help you choose events that match your skills and your bankroll.

Whether you play small daily events, a Sunday major or a series run by one of the rooms on our best poker bonuses page, the structure sheet tells you how much skill and how much luck to expect. This guide explains what the numbers mean and how to use them when deciding where to register.

The three numbers that define a tournament

Every structure can be summarised with three figures: the starting stack, the level length and the blind progression. Together they determine how many big blinds you have to work with and how quickly that number shrinks.

  • Starting stack: the number of chips every player begins with. On its own it means little, because it must be compared with the opening blinds.
  • Level length: how many minutes each blind level lasts. Longer levels give you more hands at each stack depth.
  • Blind progression: how fast the blinds rise from level to level. Gentle increases reward patient play. Steep increases force action.

The most useful single measure is starting depth, which is the starting stack divided by the opening big blind. A tournament that begins with 100 big blinds is a very different game from one that begins with 30.

Starting depth: deep, standard and turbo

As a rough guide, a start of 150 big blinds or more is considered deep, around 100 is a standard depth, and anything under 50 is a short-stack start that plays like a late stage from the first hand. Deep structures are where post-flop skill matters most. You can make a mistake early and recover, bluff effectively and avoid all-in decisions until the middle stages. Short structures compress the game and reduce the edge of the better player, because variance climbs when the stacks are shallow.

That does not make short-stack tournaments bad. They finish quickly, they suit players with limited time, and they are often cheaper to enter. But if you rely on outplaying opponents in complex pots, a deep structure is where you will earn the most per entry. If you prefer speed, see our guide to turbo poker tournament strategy for the adjustments that matter.

Level length and the number of hands you see

Level length is where structure becomes concrete. A twenty-minute level at a full table gives you roughly forty hands, and a ten-minute level gives you half that. The fewer hands you see before the blinds rise, the fewer chances you have to wait for a good spot, and the faster your effective stack shrinks.

Online, levels commonly run from five or six minutes in hyper-turbo events up to fifteen or twenty minutes in larger tournaments, and some premium events run longer. When you compare two events, calculate how many hands you can expect before the stack falls into push-or-fold territory. A structure that leaves you with a workable stack for four hours is much better for a skilled player than one that reduces you to twelve big blinds by the end of the second hour.

Antes and big blind antes

Modern online tournaments increasingly use a big blind ante, where one player posts the ante for the whole table. This speeds up the game and reduces the problem of players forgetting to post, but it also increases the cost of each orbit and changes how often late-position players should open. Our guide to big blind ante strategy covers the adjustments you need to make, and our bonus types page shows how tournament-focused offers pay out.

Look at when antes start. A structure that introduces antes early is more aggressive than one that delays them until the middle levels. Earlier antes mean larger pots, more stealing and a smaller advantage for waiting. Later antes preserve the slow, patient opening stages that suit careful players.

Late registration and re-entry

The structure also controls how late registration works. Many events allow entry for the first several levels, and some allow re-entry after busting. A long registration window can be useful if you want to join at a deeper effective stack than the average player, but late entries also mean the field is still growing and the prize pool is larger, a point we cover in our late registration analysis. Re-entry events tend to have larger fields and more aggressive early play, because players know they can buy back in.

Check how the registration window interacts with the clock. If you can register at level six and still get 60 big blinds, that is a healthy structure. If late registration leaves you with 20 big blinds, you are essentially buying a coin flip.

Comparing structures in practice

Suppose two tournaments each charge a similar buy-in. Event A gives 10,000 chips, opens at 50 and 100, and runs twenty-minute levels. Event B gives 5,000 chips, opens at 50 and 100, and runs ten-minute levels. Event A starts with 100 big blinds and gives you roughly twice as many hands at each depth. Event B starts with 50 big blinds and cuts the time you have. For almost any skilled player, Event A is the better product, even though the buy-in is the same.

The inverse is true if you are short of time. Event B might finish in half the time and give you a fair shot at the final table, which is useful for a lunch-break session. The point is not that one structure is correct, but that you should choose with your eyes open. Structure and bonus value

Structure also affects how much of a promotion you can use. A bonus that releases through tournament fees is released faster the more events you enter, so shorter structures that let you play more tournaments per hour can clear it sooner. Conversely, deep events cost more time and may give a lower fee-per-hour rate. Weigh the time you have against the structure you prefer, and remember that the best use of any promotion is playing the format you will profit from. A simple pre-registration checklist

Before you register for a tournament, check the starting depth, the level length, the ante schedule, the late registration window and the re-entry rules. If the starting depth is under 50 big blinds and the levels are short, treat it as a high-variance event. If the depth is over 100 and the levels are long, treat it as a skill-heavy event. Match that to your bankroll and goals, and you will avoid paying premium prices for a structure that does not reward your strengths.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good starting stack for an online poker tournament?

A start of around 100 big blinds or more is considered standard to deep, giving skilled players room to outplay opponents. Starts under 50 big blinds play more like late-stage tournaments and carry more variance.

Do longer blind levels make a tournament better for skilled players?

Generally yes. Longer levels give you more hands at each stack depth, which rewards post-flop skill and patience, while very short levels push the game toward shove-or-fold decisions and increase luck.
