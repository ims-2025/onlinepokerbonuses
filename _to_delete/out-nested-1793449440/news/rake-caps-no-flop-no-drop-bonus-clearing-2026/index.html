Rake Caps and No Flop No Drop: Bonus Clearing Guide

Rake caps and no-flop-no-drop rules change how much rake you generate. See how they affect bonus clearing, rakeback and which stakes are worth playing.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 26, 2026·8 min read

When players compare poker sites, they tend to focus on the headline numbers: a 100% match up to $2,000, a rakeback percentage, a weekly leaderboard. Far fewer players look at the two mechanical rules that quietly determine how much rake they pay and how fast a bonus unlocks. Those rules are the rake cap and the no-flop-no-drop policy. Understanding them can change which stakes you play, which tables you choose and how quickly you clear a welcome bonus.

This article explains both rules in plain language, shows how they interact with bonus release and rakeback, and walks through practical adjustments for cash game players in the US and Canada. Exact numbers vary by site and stake level and can change, so treat the examples here as illustrations and always confirm current rake schedules in the lobby or the room's published rules.

What a rake cap is

Rake is the fee a poker room takes from cash game pots. It is usually a percentage of the pot, up to a maximum amount per hand. That maximum is the rake cap. If a room charges 5% with a cap of $3, a $40 pot would generate $2 in rake, while a $200 pot would generate only $3 instead of $10, because the cap stops the percentage from growing indefinitely.

The cap matters because it makes big pots proportionally cheaper. Your effective rake rate falls as pots grow. At low stakes, the cap is often reached only in unusually large pots, so rake feels like a steady percentage. At higher stakes, caps are hit more often, which is one reason that the cost of rake as a share of winnings tends to shrink as you move up. For a refresher on the broader mechanics, see our guide to how poker rake works.

What no flop, no drop means

No flop, no drop is a simple policy: if the hand ends before a flop is dealt, the room takes no rake. A raise that wins the blinds preflop, a fold to a three-bet, or any other hand where everyone gives up before the flop costs nothing in fees. Most rooms that serve the US and Canada market apply this rule to cash games, and it is good news for players because it protects the large share of hands that never see a flop.

There are two consequences. First, aggressive preflop strategies such as stealing blinds and three-betting are cheaper to execute, because folds do not feed the rake. Second, a hand that never reaches the flop does not generate rake that counts toward your bonus. If your goal is clearing a bonus quickly, those preflop wins do not help. Your Award Points or equivalent come from hands where rake was actually collected.

How rake feeds your bonus

On the Winning Poker Network, welcome bonuses release in increments as you generate loyalty points tied to the rake you pay or contribute. The Americas Cardroom review covers the details of that release schedule, and our explainer on how bonuses release in increments shows how the pieces fit together. The point is that bonus clearing is driven by rake, so any rule that affects rake collection affects how fast your bonus unlocks.

A related concept is whether the room credits you for rake you are dealt or rake you actually contributed. Under dealt-rake systems, you earn a share of the rake in every pot you are dealt into regardless of whether you put in chips; under contributed systems you earn in proportion to your own contribution to the pot. Which one applies shapes how you should play, and we compare them in our article on dealt versus contributed rake.

The interaction: why caps change the best stakes for bonus clearing

Consider two players, one at low stakes and one at mid stakes, each trying to clear a bonus. At low stakes, the rake cap is rarely hit, so rake per hand is nearly proportional to pot size. Because pots are small, a single hour at a full table generates modest points. At mid stakes, pots are larger and caps are reached more often, so the rake per hand levels off, but because the cap itself is higher, rake per hour is typically greater.

That means bonus clearing is usually faster in absolute terms at higher stakes, but it comes with higher variance and a larger bankroll requirement. The trade-off is why advice to simply jump up in stakes to clear a bonus faster can be dangerous. Our guides on bankroll management for bonus clearing and moving up in stakes cover how to balance the two.

Practical adjustments for your game

  • Pick tables where pots are raised and contested. Multiway, three-bet pots generate more rake than limped pots that fold on the flop.
  • Choose full ring or 6-max thoughtfully. Full tables see more flops per hour overall, while short-handed tables see more hands per hour. Our guide to 6-max versus full ring breaks down the rake and bonus implications.
  • Consider fast-fold formats. More hands per hour means more rake paid and more points earned, as explained in our fast-fold bonus guide.
  • Use the cap in your favor. When you play deep and build large pots with strong hands, the effective rake rate drops.
  • Do not play junk to generate rake. Spewing chips to pay fees defeats the purpose, since the rake you pay is only partly refunded.

Rakeback and the effective cost of play

Rakeback programs return a portion of rake as a rebate. If your room pays 27% back and your effective rake rate is 4% of pot, your net cost drops noticeably. But caps matter here too, because rakeback is a percentage of rake paid, not of pot size. A capped pot generates less rake and thus less rakeback. To calculate your actual cost of play, use the approach in our effective rake rate calculator guide.

If you compare rooms, look at the rake schedule alongside the bonus and rakeback terms. A site with a generous match but a rake structure with no cap or a high percentage may cost more over time than one with a smaller match and gentler fees. Common misconceptions

One frequent mistake is assuming that all rake counts toward your bonus. In reality, rake collected in tournaments, in different game types or in promotional pots may release bonus at different rates or not at all. Another misconception is that higher rake always means faster bonus clearing. A high rake structure generates more points, yes, but also eats more of your bankroll. A third is believing that no flop, no drop hurts your clearing speed. It does not slow you in any meaningful way, because those hands would not have produced points under any structure.

A final misconception: that rake caps are the same everywhere. Caps vary by stake, by number of players and by room. Look at the rake table for the exact stake you play, and recheck it occasionally, since rooms adjust schedules.

Choosing a game with all of this in mind

The best game for bonus clearing is one where you are a winning or break-even player, where the rake structure is reasonable, and where you can play enough volume to unlock the bonus before it expires. Frequently asked questions

Does no flop, no drop affect how fast I clear a poker bonus?

No. Hands that end before the flop produce no rake, so they would not have earned bonus points anyway. Your clearing speed depends on rake collected in hands that reach the flop.

Do higher stakes clear bonuses faster because of the rake cap?

Often yes in absolute terms, since pots are larger and the cap is higher, but variance and bankroll requirements rise too. Only move up if your bankroll supports it.
