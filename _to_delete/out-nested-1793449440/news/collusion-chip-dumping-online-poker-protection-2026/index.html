Poker Collusion & Chip Dumping: How to Spot and Report It

Collusion and chip dumping hurt honest players. Learn the warning signs, how poker rooms detect them, and how to report suspicious play to support.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 29, 2026·8 min read

Every online poker player eventually wonders whether a strange hand was just bad luck or something less innocent. Most of the time it is variance. Occasionally, though, two or more players are working together against the table, and that is called collusion. Closely related is chip dumping, where one account deliberately loses chips to another. Both practices are against the rules at every reputable room, and both damage trust in the games you are trying to beat.

This article explains what collusion and chip dumping actually look like, why they happen, how rooms detect them, and what you can do as a player. If you are choosing where to deposit, understanding game integrity is just as important as comparing a headline offer on our best poker bonuses page.

What counts as collusion?

Collusion is any form of cooperation between players that gives them an unfair advantage over the rest of the table. The classic version involves two friends sitting at the same cash game, sharing hole card information through chat apps or a phone call, and using that information to trap a third player in a raising war. In tournaments, players may agree to avoid betting against each other, to gang up on a short stack, or to manipulate the bubble.

Common forms include:

  • Sharing hole cards or strategy in real time while both players are in the same hand.
  • Whipsawing, where two colluders raise and re-raise to trap a victim between them.
  • Soft play, where partners decline to bet or raise against each other even with strong hands.
  • Chip dumping, where one account intentionally loses a pot to move money or tournament chips to another.
  • Account sharing or multi-accounting at the same table, which effectively lets one person play several hands.

Not everything that looks suspicious is cheating. Two players who know each other and avoid fighting each other for a few hands may simply be playing tight. The difference is pattern and intent, which is why rooms look at large samples rather than a single hand.

Why chip dumping happens

Chip dumping has several motives. In tournaments, a player who has been eliminated may pass chips to a teammate to improve their stack and finishing position. In cash games, the practice can be used to move money between accounts to launder funds, to bypass withdrawal limits, or to cash out bonus money in ways the rules do not allow. Because bonuses often have playthrough or contribution requirements, some cheaters try to generate artificial rake or release bonus increments by dumping chips among their own accounts, which is exactly why rooms watch these patterns closely.

That is also the reason bonus terms often include language about abusive play. Our guide on how poker bonuses are claimed and released explains why you should always read the fine print: a bonus can be forfeited if the room believes the rake was generated through improper activity, even if you personally did nothing wrong at the table.

How poker rooms detect cheating

Large online poker rooms have security teams and automated systems whose job is to find unnatural patterns. While each room guards its methods, the general techniques are well understood in the industry. Hand-history analysis looks for accounts that play unusually passively against each other or that fold winning hands. Statistical tools compare actual results to expected outcomes. Rooms also review how often two accounts sit together, whether they enter the same tournaments, and how their stack sizes move over time.

Technical signals matter too. Multiple accounts logging in from the same device, network, or location may trigger a manual review. Payment data, such as many accounts withdrawing to the same wallet or cashier method, can be an even stronger clue. Crypto is not a loophole here; blockchain transactions are public, and a pattern of related wallets is easy to investigate. If you use Bitcoin or another coin for deposits, keep your own records tidy and your accounts strictly separate.

Most rooms also run identity checks before cashouts, which makes it harder for a person to operate numerous accounts. That is one reason documents are requested, and it is covered in more detail in our reviews of rooms such as Americas Cardroom and BetOnline Poker.

Warning signs at the table

You will never be certain from a handful of hands, but certain patterns deserve attention. Watch for two players who consistently avoid raising each other, especially from the blinds or in late position where aggression would normally be expected. Be suspicious if a pair repeatedly whipsaws the same victim, if one player makes bizarre folds against their partner, or if a short stack suddenly jams into a single opponent and loses without any logic.

In tournaments, look for chip movement toward one account near the money or at the final table, and pay attention to how players behave when they are eliminated. A player who loses a big pot and then logs off instantly while the winner stays in the event for no apparent reason may be part of a dump. Keep your suspicions practical rather than accusatory, and take notes rather than confronting anyone at the table.

How to report suspicious play

If you believe you have seen collusion or chip dumping, the best step is to contact support and give them evidence they can act on. A vague complaint is hard to investigate, while a clear report with details is far more useful. Include:

  1. The date, time zone, table or tournament name, and the screen names involved.
  2. Hand numbers or hand-history text for the hands that concern you.
  3. A short description of why the action looked unusual, avoiding speculation about motive.
  4. Any screenshots that show chip counts, seating, or chat behavior.

Do not publicly name or accuse players in forums before the room has finished reviewing. You may be wrong, and public accusations can create legal and reputational problems. Rooms rarely share the outcome of an investigation, so do not be discouraged if you do not receive a detailed reply. A response saying the matter has been forwarded to the security team is normal.

What honest players can do to protect themselves

You cannot eliminate every risk, but you can reduce it. Choose rooms with a track record of responsive support and clear rules about multi-accounting and bonus abuse. Avoid sitting in a game that you suspect is infested with colluders, and use table selection to find softer, more natural games. Our look at offshore poker for US and Canada players explains how licensing and network size affect the player pool you will face.

Larger networks generally have more liquidity, which makes it harder for a small group to dominate a table. Smaller rooms can be fine, but they offer cheaters fewer places to hide only if they have strong monitoring. Wherever you play, keep your own account secure with a unique password and two-factor authentication where available, because compromised accounts are a different but related integrity problem.

Staying realistic about bad beats

It is natural to look for explanations when you lose, but a few hours of poker is a tiny sample. Even the best hands lose frequently, and many suspicious-looking hands are simply bad play by weak opponents. Before you report anything, ask whether the action makes sense for a recreational player, whether it repeated over many hands, and whether the same accounts are involved. Evidence beats intuition every time.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between collusion and chip dumping?

Collusion is any secret cooperation between players to gain an unfair edge, while chip dumping is a specific act where one account deliberately loses chips to another. Chip dumping is often one tool used within a collusion scheme.

Can I get in trouble if I report a hand that turns out to be innocent?

No. Reporting concerns in good faith with factual details is fine and helps security teams. Just avoid publicly accusing other players, because you may not have the full picture.
