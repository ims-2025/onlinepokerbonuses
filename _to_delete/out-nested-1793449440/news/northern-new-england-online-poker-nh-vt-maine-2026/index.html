Online Poker in NH, Vermont and Maine: Legal Status 2026

Can you play online poker for real money in New Hampshire, Vermont or Maine? See what the law says, how offshore rooms fit in, and how to stay safe.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 26, 2026·8 min read

Northern New England is a beautiful place to live, but it is not a place where the online poker landscape has been easy to follow. New Hampshire, Vermont and Maine have small populations, modest gaming industries and a long tradition of letting larger states lead on new forms of gambling. Players in these states who want to sit down for a real-money game online often find confusing, contradictory information, and it is no surprise that many ask the same question: is it legal, and where can I actually play?

This guide explains the general legal picture for the three states, how it differs from the handful of states with licensed in-state poker, and what practical options exist for players who want action. It is educational information rather than legal advice, and laws change, so always confirm the current rules with official state sources or a qualified attorney before you deposit. For a wider overview of the national landscape, see our US poker guide.

The big picture: no state-licensed online poker in these three states

As of this writing, none of New Hampshire, Vermont or Maine operates a state-licensed, regulated online poker market of the kind found in states such as New Jersey, Pennsylvania or Michigan. Those licensed markets exist because their legislatures passed specific statutes and regulators issued licenses to operators. In northern New England, lawmakers have discussed the idea from time to time, but the small player base makes it difficult to justify a standalone market, and the states have generally focused their gambling policy on lotteries, charitable gaming, retail casinos and sports betting.

That is an important distinction. The absence of a licensed market does not automatically mean that an individual player breaks a specific law by playing online, and it also does not mean that online poker is formally approved. It simply means there is no regulated in-state option, and that residents who want to play online typically look to sites based outside the United States. Our explainer on regulated US states versus offshore rooms lays out the trade-offs in detail.

New Hampshire

New Hampshire is best known for its state-run lottery, which has operated for decades and was among the first in the country. The state also allows charitable gaming, including charitable poker rooms run under a licensing framework, and legalized sports wagering, with mobile betting available through a state-lottery partnership. That history makes New Hampshire comfortable with some forms of gambling, but it has not created a licensed online poker market. If you live in New Hampshire, you will not find a state-regulated poker app, and the state lottery's online presence focuses on lottery games rather than card rooms.

Vermont

Vermont has one of the most conservative gambling environments in the country. Its lottery is modest, there are no commercial casinos, and the state only recently moved toward legal sports betting. There is no licensed online poker framework, and the state's policy discussions have centered more on sports wagering and responsible-gaming safeguards than on card rooms. Vermont players who want to play online poker are, in practice, dealing with a market that exists entirely outside the state's regulatory structure.

Maine

Maine permits a handful of casinos, a state lottery and mobile sports betting in partnership with the state's tribal nations. The tribal gaming landscape adds a layer of complexity, since agreements between tribes and the state shape what types of gaming can occur. Despite those developments, Maine has not launched a regulated online poker product. Players in the state follow the same pattern as their neighbors: no in-state licensed poker, and a reliance on rooms that serve the US market from offshore.

How federal law fits in

Federal law shapes the discussion across all three states. The Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act targets payment processing for illegal internet gambling, while the Wire Act has long been debated in connection with interstate gambling transactions. Neither law was designed to criminalize an individual recreational player, and enforcement efforts have historically focused on operators and payment processors. We break down the interplay in our explainer on the Wire Act and UIGEA. That said, the legal status of offshore play is a gray area, and you should make your own informed decision.

Risks and protections to keep in mind

Playing at an offshore room means you do not have the consumer protections that a state regulator provides. There is no state gaming commission to file a complaint with, and disputes must be settled through the operator's support channels or the licensing body in the jurisdiction where the site is registered. That is why choosing carefully matters.

  • Check the operator's track record. Look for years of payouts, active player communities and consistent cashout speeds.
  • Read the bonus terms before you accept any offer, especially wagering and release schedules.
  • Start small. Make a modest first deposit and complete a test withdrawal before committing a larger bankroll.
  • Complete verification early. Submitting identity documents before you request a cashout avoids delays.

What might change

Legal landscapes evolve. Multi-state compacts such as the Multi-State Internet Gaming Agreement let regulated states share player pools, and our overview of shared liquidity explains how that works. Small states like Vermont and New Hampshire could theoretically benefit from joining such a compact someday, but no such move has materialized, and there is no guarantee one will. In the meantime, keep an eye on your state legislature's announcements and our US legal updates for any developments.

If you live in New Hampshire, Vermont or Maine, there is no licensed in-state online poker site, and the practical choice is between not playing and using an offshore room at your own risk.
