BB/100 Win Rate Explained: Measure Your Poker Results

Learn what bb/100 means, how to calculate it, how much volume you need for a reliable number, and how bonuses and rakeback change your true win rate.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 31, 2026·7 min read

If you play online cash games for any length of time, you will hear players talk about their bb/100. It is the standard yardstick for measuring how well you beat a given stake, and it is the number serious players use to decide when to move up, when to move down and whether a game is worth their time. Despite its popularity, it is also one of the most misunderstood statistics in poker, often quoted without the context that makes it meaningful.

This guide explains what bb/100 means, how to calculate it, why a small sample can mislead you, and how rakeback and a poker bonus change the number that really matters: your all-in profit per hundred hands.

What bb/100 actually measures

The abbreviation stands for big blinds won per 100 hands. If you win 500 big blinds over 10,000 hands, your win rate is 5 bb/100, because 10,000 hands is 100 blocks of 100 hands and you earned 5 big blinds on average per block. Using big blinds as the unit lets you compare results across different stakes: winning 5 bb/100 at $0.50/$1 is the same skill level as winning 5 bb/100 at $2/$4, even though the dollar amounts differ.

For tournaments, the equivalent measure is return on investment, or ROI, which compares profit to buy-ins. Cash-game players use bb/100 because they play a continuous stream of hands and want a rate that reflects performance over time. Our article on tournament ROI, variance and sample size covers the tournament side of this idea.

How to calculate your win rate

The calculation is straightforward. Take your total profit in dollars, divide by the big blind size to convert to big blinds, then divide by the number of hands played and multiply by 100. If you made $300 over 20,000 hands at $0.25/$0.50, the big blind is $0.50, so you won 600 big blinds. Divide 600 by 20,000 and multiply by 100, and your win rate is 3 bb/100.

  • Step one: convert profit into big blinds by dividing by the big blind.
  • Step two: divide the result by hands played.
  • Step three: multiply by 100 to express it per hundred hands.
  • Step four: separate results before and after rakeback so you know your true edge from play alone.

Most tracking programs and many poker clients do this automatically. If you track by hand, a spreadsheet is enough, and our guide to a bonus progress tracker shows how to log sessions, hands and rewards in one place.

What counts as a good win rate

Win rates vary widely by stake, format and table selection. At micro and small stakes, a solid regular might win somewhere around 3 to 8 bb/100 in 6-max cash games, while full-ring games often produce lower numbers because the pots are played with more opponents. At higher stakes, a win rate of 2 to 4 bb/100 can be excellent, since the player pool is tougher and the margins are thinner.

These ranges are rough guides, not guarantees. A high win rate at a soft table does not necessarily carry over to a tougher lineup, and a modest rate at a tough table can still be impressive. Table selection is one of the most powerful tools for raising your win rate, and our piece on table selection and softer games explains how to choose seats that suit your edge.

Why sample size matters

Poker results are noisy. Even a strong player can run well below expectation for tens of thousands of hands, and a weak player can run hot for just as long. The standard deviation of a typical no-limit hold'em player is large, often in the neighborhood of 60 to 100 bb per 100 hands, which means your observed win rate can swing dramatically in small samples.

As a rule of thumb, you need at least 50,000 to 100,000 hands before your observed win rate says anything reliable, and even then the confidence interval is wide. If you have played 5,000 hands and you are running at 15 bb/100, you are probably not a 15 bb/100 player; you are a player who has been fortunate over a short stretch. The same logic applies in reverse when you are losing. Our guide on poker downswings and variance math offers a deeper look at how long cold streaks can last.

Winnings before and after rakeback

One reason serious players track bb/100 carefully is that rakeback and bonus income can turn a marginal win rate into a profitable one. If you play a game where you break even before rewards, the rakeback you earn can be the entire margin. Imagine you play 100,000 hands and pay a certain amount in rake; getting a portion of that rake returned increases your profit even if your play does not change.

Track two figures: your raw win rate from play, and your adjusted win rate including rakeback and any released bonus. The raw number tells you how good you are at the game, while the adjusted number tells you how profitable your sessions are in practice. Both matter, but mixing them can give you a misleading picture of your skill and lead you to move up before you are ready.

Using bb/100 to make bankroll decisions

Your win rate and your standard deviation together determine how large a bankroll you need. A higher win rate lets you handle more variance, while a lower rate demands a bigger cushion. Many players keep 20 to 40 buy-ins for their stake, then move up when they have a comfortable margin and a sample large enough to trust.

Do not move up on the strength of a hot streak. If your win rate at the next stake would be lower because of tougher opponents, then jumping up too soon can erase your cushion. Our article on when to move up stakes lays out practical rules that account for both skill and bankroll.

Common mistakes when reading win rates

  • Judging yourself on a few thousand hands and mistaking luck for skill.
  • Comparing win rates across game types, such as 6-max and full-ring, without adjusting for context.
  • Ignoring all-in luck, which can distort short samples. Adjusted all-in results can help, although they are not perfect.
  • Counting bonus money as skill-based profit, which hides leaks in your actual play.
  • Changing stakes or formats so often that no single sample is large enough to analyze.

Turning data into a plan

A win rate is only useful if it drives decisions. Review your numbers monthly, split them by stake, position and game type, and look for patterns. If you lose money from the blinds or in 3-bet pots, that is where to focus study time. If your win rate is steady but your volume is low, you may be leaving rakeback and bonus value on the table.

Frequently asked questions

How many hands do I need to trust my bb/100?

Plan on at least 50,000 to 100,000 hands for a rough estimate. Even then the margin of error is wide, so treat the number as a range rather than a precise figure.

Should I include rakeback in my win rate?

Track both. Your raw win rate shows your skill, while the adjusted number including rakeback and released bonuses shows your real profit per hundred hands.
