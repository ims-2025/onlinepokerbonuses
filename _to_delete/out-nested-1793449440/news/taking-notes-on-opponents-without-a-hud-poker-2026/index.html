Taking Notes on Opponents in Online Poker Without a HUD

No HUD? No problem. Learn a simple, rules-friendly system for taking opponent notes in online poker and using them to win more at low and micro stakes.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 28, 2026·8 min read

Many online poker rooms limit or ban third-party tracking software, and some formats, such as anonymous tables, remove the opponent information entirely. That leaves one of the oldest tools in poker: the note. Written observations about how specific opponents play remain legal, free and effective, and at low and micro stakes they can be worth more than any statistic.

This guide shows you how to build a simple note-taking habit that fits into real play, which observations matter most and how to turn raw notes into better decisions. It pairs well with our overview of HUD and tracking software rules, and it works whether you are clearing a welcome bonus or simply improving your win rate.

Why notes still matter

At low stakes, many players are recreational, they play inconsistently, and they return to the same tables. A single strong observation, such as someone who never folds a pair or someone who bluffs every missed draw, can be exploited for hours. Statistics need a large sample to be reliable, but a vivid showdown can reveal a lot immediately. Notes capture the story behind the numbers.

Notes also help in the formats where software is restricted. Some rooms use anonymous or randomized screen names to prevent data mining, as discussed in our guide to anonymous poker tables. In those games, you may not be able to track a player across sessions, but you can still track them within a session, which is often enough.

Check the rules first

Before relying on any tool, read the room's terms. Built-in note features are allowed at essentially every room we cover, including BetOnline Poker and Black Chip Poker, but rules on external software, screen sharing and outside assistance vary. Taking notes by hand, in the room's own note feature or in a simple text file on your own device is generally acceptable, but when in doubt, ask support and keep the answer in writing.

What to record

The goal is not to write an essay about every opponent. The goal is to capture the few facts that change your decisions. Use short labels and focus on showdowns and surprising actions, since those are the moments that reveal real ranges.

  • Preflop tendencies. Does the player open wide or tight, limp often or three-bet aggressively? A label such as 'loose opener' or 'never three-bets bluffs' is enough.
  • Postflop aggression. Note whether they c-bet every flop, give up easily or raise draws. Compare this with our continuation betting guide.
  • Showdown surprises. Write down any hand that does not match the action, such as a check-raise with a weak pair or a river bluff with air.
  • Calling patterns. Does the player call too much, or fold to a second bet? This tells you how to size value bets and bluffs.
  • Bet-sizing patterns. Many players use sizes that correlate with hand strength. Our article on bet sizing tells explains how to spot them.
  • Emotional state. A player who just lost a big pot may be on tilt, which changes how they play for a while.

A simple labeling system

Consistency matters more than detail. Develop a small set of labels you can apply in a few seconds. A common approach uses categories like tight-passive, loose-passive (calling station), tight-aggressive, loose-aggressive and maniac, combined with a short note about the key exploit.

For example, a loose-passive player who calls down with any pair should be value bet thinly and rarely bluffed. Our guide to exploiting calling stations shows how to profit from that profile. A hyper-aggressive player who bets and raises constantly is better handled by letting them bluff into your strong hands, as described in playing against maniacs.

Color-coding, if the software supports it, speeds things up. Use one color for players to target, another for players to avoid bluffing and a third for those who are dangerous. Keep the system simple enough that you can read it at a glance while multi-tabling.

Taking notes while playing

Time is limited, especially at fast tables. Do not try to write full notes while the action is live. Instead, use a two-step approach: tag the player quickly with a label during the hand, then add detail during a break or between hands. If you play several tables, our multi-tabling guide explains how to manage attention without sacrificing decisions.

Prioritize the biggest pots and the strangest plays. If you only note two things per session, make them a hand that surprised you and a pattern you saw repeated. Over weeks, that small habit builds an impressive library.

Turning notes into decisions

Notes only matter if they change your play. Before each session, review the notes on regular opponents. During play, ask a simple question at key decisions: what does my note say about this player? If a player is known to bluff rivers, widen your calling range. If they only bet the river with strong hands, fold more often. Our guides to bluff catching and hand reading help you convert reads into ranges.

Be careful about overreacting to small samples. One bluff does not make a player a maniac. Treat early notes as hypotheses and update them as evidence accumulates. A note that says 'possible bluffer, seen once' is more honest, and more useful, than 'always bluffs'.

Pair notes with a results review

At the end of a session, review hands where your read was tested. Did you fold a hand that your note suggested calling? Did you call a bet you should have respected? A short review habit, as described in our guide to tracking poker results and session review, accelerates improvement. If a particular player keeps appearing at your stakes, you can also change table selection, a topic covered in our table selection guide.

Keep notes private and secure

Your notes are part of your edge, so treat them like any valuable file. Do not share them in public chats, and keep backups if you use a text file. Never include personal information about opponents, only observations about how they play. If you use a device that also holds your gaming accounts, protect it with a strong password and two-factor authentication, as outlined in our account security guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is taking notes on opponents allowed at online poker rooms?

Using the room's built-in note feature or private notes on your own device is generally allowed, but rules on external software vary, so check the room's terms.

What should I write in an opponent note?

Focus on showdown surprises, preflop looseness, aggression, calling tendencies and bet-sizing patterns, using short labels so you can read them quickly.
