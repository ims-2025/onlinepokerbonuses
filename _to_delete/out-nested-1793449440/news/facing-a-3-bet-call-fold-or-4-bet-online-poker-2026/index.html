Facing a 3-Bet: Call, Fold or 4-Bet in Online Poker

Opened and got 3-bet? Learn when to call, fold or 4-bet online, how position and sizing shape your defense, and why rake changes the math at every stake.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 23, 2026·8 min read

You open the cutoff with a hand you like, the button re-raises, and the action is back on you with a decision that will define a large part of your results. Facing a 3-bet is one of the most common high-leverage spots in online poker, and it is also one of the most commonly mishandled. Players either fold far too often and get run over by aggressive opponents, or they call too wide out of position and bleed chips on every later street. Getting this spot right is worth more to your win rate than almost any postflop trick.

This guide gives you a practical framework you can use at the tables on any of the rooms we cover, whether you are grinding micro stakes while working through a welcome offer from our best poker bonuses list or playing mid-stakes cash games for a living. We will cover how to think about ranges, why position dominates the decision, how to size a 4-bet, and how rake quietly shifts the math.

Start with the opponent, not your cards

The right response to a 3-bet depends first on who made it. A player who 3-bets four percent of the time has a very different range from one who 3-bets twelve percent. Against a tight 3-bettor, their range is dominated by premium pairs and ace-king, so you should fold most of your marginal hands and continue only with hands that play well against a strong range. Against a loose 3-bettor, their range contains many bluffs and thin value hands, and you can defend much wider, mostly by calling in position and 4-betting a polarized selection.

At smaller stakes you rarely have large samples, so lean on simple reads. If a player has 3-bet you twice in ten minutes from the blinds, they are probably not holding premium hands every time. If a passive player who has barely raised all session suddenly 3-bets, believe them. Population tendencies matter too: at the lowest stakes, 3-bets are usually stronger than the math says they should be, while at higher stakes with regular opponents they are more balanced and more frequent.

Position decides how wide you can call

Calling a 3-bet in position is a very different proposition from calling out of position. When you have the button against a 3-bet from the blinds, you realize your equity much more often. You act last on every postflop street, can check back to control the pot, and can pressure a player who fires one barrel and gives up. Hands like suited connectors, suited broadways, and mid pocket pairs gain real value in this spot.

Out of position, the picture flips. If you open from the button and the big blind 3-bets, you are the one who acts last, so you are in good shape. But if you open the hijack and the button 3-bets, you will be out of position for the entire hand. Calling with speculative hands here is usually a mistake, because you will rarely get to see cheap showdowns. A good rule of thumb is to tighten your calling range substantially whenever the 3-bettor has position, and to prefer 4-betting or folding over flat calling with marginal holdings.

Building your 4-bet range

A 4-bet range has two parts: value hands and bluffs. For value, your premium pairs and ace-king belong there almost always, with queens and ace-king being the borderline group that depends on the opponent. For bluffs, the best candidates are hands that block your opponent's strong holdings and still have some playability if called. Suited aces such as ace-five suited are classic choices because the ace removes one of the cards that makes up aces, ace-king and ace-queen, and the suit gives you backup equity.

Avoid bluff 4-betting with hands that have real calling value. Hands like jack-ten suited or pocket sevens do better as calls in position because they keep dominated opponents in the pot and still realize equity. The goal is to pick bluffs that gain the most from folding out better hands and lose the least when called. If you are unsure, a small number of well-chosen bluffs beats a large number of poorly-chosen ones.

Sizing your 4-bet

Sizing should reflect position and stack depth. A common approach at 100 big blinds is to make your 4-bet around two and a quarter times the 3-bet when you are in position, and closer to two and a half times, or slightly more, when you are out of position. The larger size when out of position is meant to deny the opponent the right price to call and to make your continuing decisions on later streets simpler.

At shorter stack depths the calculus changes. With stacks near forty or fifty big blinds, a 4-bet often commits you, and the cleanest play is frequently to jam with your value hands and your best bluffs and fold the rest. The extra step of a small 4-bet followed by a fold to a 5-bet is expensive and rarely needed. Pay attention to effective stack size before every decision, because the same hand can be a clear call at one depth and a clear fold at another.

How rake changes the decision

Rake matters more than most players realize in 3-bet pots. In raked games with no flop, no drop rules, you contribute to the rake only when a flop is seen, but once a 3-bet pot is called the pot is large and the rake taken can be a meaningful percentage of your expected profit. Marginal calls that look break-even in a solver without rake often become losing plays when you subtract the house's cut. This is one reason that tight, well-structured calling ranges outperform loose ones at low and micro stakes where the rake percentage is highest.

Common leaks to fix

  • Calling out of position with weak suited hands and then folding to a continuation bet, which wastes the chips you already invested.
  • Never 4-betting as a bluff, which lets aggressive players 3-bet you at will with no fear.
  • Over-folding to 3-bets from the blinds, which makes it profitable for opponents to attack your late-position opens.
  • Making 4-bets so small that opponents get excellent odds to call with a wide range.
  • Ignoring stack depth and using the same plan at 40 big blinds as at 200.

A simple plan you can apply tonight

Build your defense in three buckets. First, a value 4-bet group of premium pairs and ace-king. Second, a calling group made of hands that play well, such as medium pairs and strong suited broadways, mostly when you have position. Third, a bluff 4-bet group of a few suited aces. Everything else folds. Review your sessions afterward and look specifically at hands where you faced a 3-bet, because that sample will reveal your biggest leaks quickly.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I fold to a 3-bet?

It depends on the opponent and position, but a typical baseline is folding roughly half to six-tenths of your opening range when facing a 3-bet, folding less in position and against loose 3-bettors.

Is it ever right to flat call a 3-bet out of position?

Yes, but with a narrow range. Strong hands that you want to keep dominated opponents in with, such as pairs like jacks or tens against a wide range, can call, while most speculative hands are better folded or used as 4-bet bluffs.
