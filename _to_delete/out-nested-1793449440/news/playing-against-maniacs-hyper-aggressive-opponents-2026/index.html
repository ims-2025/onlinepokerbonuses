Playing Against Maniacs: Beat Hyper-Aggressive Players

Learn how to beat maniacs online: widen your calling ranges, trap with strong hands, control the pot with position and avoid tilt versus aggression.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 23, 2026·8 min read

Every regular player knows the feeling of sitting down at a table and finding a maniac two seats to the left. They raise nearly every pot, fire three streets with air, and seem utterly unconcerned about the size of their stack. It is tempting to see these opponents as a nuisance, but a hyper-aggressive player is usually one of the most profitable opponents you will ever face, provided you adjust your strategy instead of fighting fire with fire.

This article explains how to recognize a maniac, which parts of your strategy to change, and how to protect your mindset while you collect chips. The same ideas work at the micro stakes tables on any of the rooms we cover, from the Winning Poker Network to the Chico Network, and they pair nicely with a welcome offer from our best poker bonuses page because tables with wild players tend to generate lots of action and rake.

Spotting a true maniac

Not every aggressive player is a maniac. A tight-aggressive regular raises with strong ranges and folds to resistance. A maniac shows a very high rate of voluntarily putting chips in preflop, raises or re-raises a large share of those hands, and continues betting on later streets without regard for the board. If your tracking software shows a voluntary participation rate above forty percent with a raise percentage near the same figure, you have found one.

Even without software, you can read the signs. They open or 3-bet with a wide variety of hands, show down weak holdings after betting every street, and rarely fold when re-raised. Take notes the first time you see a bluff on the river, because a note like fired three streets with a gutshot will help you later when you have to decide whether to call.

Widen your calling range and let them bluff

The single most effective adjustment is to call more often with hands that have showdown value. A maniac bluffs far more than the typical player, so medium-strength hands such as top pair with a middling kicker, second pair and small pocket pairs go up substantially in value. Where you would fold against a tight opponent, you can often call down against the maniac and be well ahead of their range.

This does not mean calling with everything. Choose hands that can withstand scrutiny: pairs, strong draws and hands that block your opponent's value combinations. And pay attention to sizing. Many maniacs use big bets, so the price you are getting matters, but with a range that is heavy on bluffs you can pay off wider than the pot odds alone suggest.

Trap with your strong hands

When a maniac is going to bet for you, there is little reason to bet your strong hands yourself. Check and let them fire. If you flop a set or two pair out of position, a check-call line often earns more over three streets than a lead, because the maniac will keep betting and you disguise your hand. Check-raising also becomes powerful, since an aggressive opponent is more likely to call or re-raise with weak holdings.

Be careful, though. On wet boards where many draws are possible, giving a free card can be costly. Consider the texture: on a dry flop you can happily trap, while on a coordinated flop you may want to bet or check-raise to charge draws. Our guide to wet and dry board textures goes deeper on how to choose.

Control the pot with position

Playing in position against a maniac is a big advantage because you see what they do first. If you can, sit directly to their left so that you act after them on most hands. If you cannot choose your seat, you can ask to move once a seat opens. Having position lets you call with strong holdings, raise when they show weakness and fold cheaply when they bet big into a board that missed you.

Out of position against a maniac, the main goal is to avoid building huge pots with marginal hands. A hyper-aggressive player will punish a weak, passive line by betting every street, so when you hold a hand you can call down with, decide early on your plan rather than reacting street by street.

Adjust your preflop strategy

Against a player who constantly raises, your 3-bet range should become more value-heavy. Hands such as ace-king, pocket pairs and strong broadways are great 3-bets because the maniac will call or 4-bet with plenty of worse. Bluff 3-bets lose value since the opponent rarely folds. Instead of trying to push them off hands, aim to get your chips in with the best of it. If you want a refresher on handling aggression before the flop, read our article on facing a 3-bet.

Be ready to isolate. If the maniac opens and others fold to you, a raise to isolate them often produces a heads-up pot in which you have position and a stronger range. Avoid multiway pots where the maniac's random raises can squeeze you out of equity.

Protect your bankroll and your mind

Variance is higher against a maniac because pots are bigger and the swings can be vicious. Make sure you are playing within your bankroll, sit with a full buy-in so you can cover the maniac's stack, and be prepared for stretches in which they hit their draws. If you find yourself getting frustrated, take a break. Tilt is the biggest way a maniac can beat a better player. Our guide to tilt control and session length offers practical routines.

  • Buy in for the full amount so you can maximize profit when you hold the best hand.
  • Keep notes on what you see at showdown and use them when making calling decisions.
  • Stay at the table only while you are playing your best; leave when focus slips.
  • Avoid playing at stakes where a swing would affect your bankroll rules.

When the maniac is not the problem

Sometimes a table with a maniac attracts several other aggressive players, and the dynamic becomes a free-for-all. In that case, consider whether the table is still the best one available. Table selection is one of the biggest edges a player can have, and a table that previously offered great action can turn into a tough one when the wrong mix of players sits down.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if an opponent is a maniac?

Look for a very high rate of entering pots and raising, frequent three-street bluffs and a tendency to show down weak hands. A voluntary participation rate above roughly 40% with an equally high raise rate is a strong sign.

Should I bluff a maniac?

Rarely. Maniacs call and raise too often to fold, so bluffing usually loses money. Focus on value betting your strong hands and calling down with solid showdown value.
