When to Move Up Stakes in Poker: Bankroll Rules for 2026

Moving up too early wrecks bankrolls; waiting too long stalls growth. Learn practical bankroll thresholds, shot-taking rules and when to drop back down.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 14, 2026·8 min read

Every winning online poker player eventually faces the same question: when is it time to move up? Stay at one level too long and your hourly rate stalls while your skills outgrow the games. Jump too soon and a normal downswing can erase months of progress. Good bankroll rules do not remove that tension, but they turn a gut-feel decision into something measurable.

This guide lays out practical thresholds for cash games and tournaments, explains how to take a controlled shot at higher stakes, and shows when moving back down is the smart play. Why moving up is not just about skill

Many players think of stakes as a ladder you climb when you are good enough. In reality, three separate ingredients decide whether a move makes sense: your bankroll, your win rate at the current level, and the quality of the games at the next one.

Bankroll comes first because variance is unavoidable. Even a strong winner can lose for tens of thousands of hands, and our guide to downswings and variance shows how long those stretches can be. A bankroll is the buffer that lets your skill show up over a large sample. Win rate matters because a player who barely beats the rake gains little from moving up. And game quality matters because the jump from the weakest to the next-weakest field is sometimes smaller than the jump in buy-ins.

Common bankroll guidelines for cash games

There is no single correct number, but widely used rules of thumb give a useful starting point. Exact figures vary by format, game selection and risk tolerance, so treat the following as approximate:

  • No-limit hold'em, six-max: many players use roughly 20 to 30 buy-ins (a buy-in being 100 big blinds) before playing a level, with more conservative players preferring 30 or more.
  • Full ring: slightly fewer buy-ins are often acceptable because variance is typically lower.
  • Pot-limit Omaha: variance is considerably higher, so 40 or more buy-ins is a common suggestion.
  • Heads-up and short-handed formats: swings are larger, and a deeper cushion is wise.

A simple way to apply this: take your current bankroll, divide by the buy-in at the next level, and ask whether the result clears your personal threshold. If you play at 100 big blinds with a 25-buy-in rule and a $2 big blind game needs a $200 buy-in, you would want about $5,000 before moving to that level. The point is not the exact multiple; it is having a rule you committed to before emotions get involved.

Tournament bankroll rules are stricter

Tournaments carry far more variance than cash games because payouts are top-heavy and a large share of players finish with nothing. A player with a genuine edge can still run through hundreds of buy-ins without a deep run, particularly in big-field events.

For that reason, tournament players often keep 100 buy-ins or more for regular multi-table events, and considerably more for large-field or high-variance formats. Smaller fields and sit-and-gos allow shallower cushions, which is one reason many beginners start with sit-and-gos. Whatever number you choose, count your average buy-in including rebuys, since a rebuy event can cost multiples of the listed price.

Look at your results, but not too closely

Before moving up, check that you are actually a winner at your current level. That means tracking your results honestly, ideally across tens of thousands of hands for cash games. Tracking software or even a basic spreadsheet is enough; our guide to tracking your results covers what to record.

Be cautious about drawing conclusions from small samples. A few thousand hands of strong results can be pure luck, while a long losing stretch may hide solid play. Rather than staring at profit, review all-in adjusted results and spot-check hands with a coach or study partner to confirm your decisions are sound. A sustained win rate combined with confidence in your fundamentals is a better signal than a hot month.

How to take a shot at higher stakes

Sometimes you want to test a level before fully committing. A shot is a limited-risk attempt at higher stakes, and the discipline lies in the limits you set before sitting down. A typical framework looks like this:

  1. Set a stop-loss in buy-ins. Decide in advance how many buy-ins you are prepared to lose at the new level, commonly two to four, before returning to your regular game.
  2. Limit the sessions. Play a defined number of sessions or hands so you gather meaningful information without exposing the bankroll to an extended run of bad luck.
  3. Use a separate allocation. Fund the shot with a portion of your roll that you can afford to lose, not with money earmarked for your main game.
  4. Review the games. Note whether opponents are making noticeably fewer mistakes, and whether your decisions feel comfortable or forced.

If the shot goes well and your bankroll reaches the full threshold for the new level, move up permanently. If it does not, drop back without ego. Treating the exercise as data collection rather than a verdict on your ability makes it much easier to stay objective.

Knowing when to move down

Most players plan how to move up and forget to plan how to move down. A good bankroll policy defines both. A common rule is to move down a level when your bankroll falls below a set number of buy-ins, such as 20 buy-ins if you moved up at 25. That buffer protects you from spiralling losses, and it keeps tilt from pushing you into bigger games to win your money back.

Moving down is not failure. Strong players routinely drop a level during bad variance or when they notice their play deteriorating, then climb again once their roll recovers. Our guide to tilt control explains why protecting your mental state is part of protecting your bankroll.

A simple moving-up checklist

  • You have at least your chosen number of buy-ins for the next level, counting only money you can truly risk.
  • You are a demonstrated winner at your current stakes over a meaningful sample.
  • You have a written stop-loss and a clear plan for moving back down.
  • You have reviewed hands against the stronger competition you expect to face.
  • Your life situation, income and stress levels allow you to absorb variance without pressure.

The bottom line

Moving up stakes should be a rules-based decision, not a mood. Set thresholds in buy-ins, confirm that you are beating your current game, take shots with strict limits, and be as willing to move down as you are to move up. Do that consistently and your bankroll grows through skill rather than being exposed to unnecessary risk.

Frequently asked questions

How many buy-ins do I need to move up in poker cash games?

Many players use roughly 20 to 30 buy-ins for no-limit hold'em and more for Omaha, though the right number depends on your win rate and risk tolerance.

Should I take shots at higher stakes?

Shots can be useful if you set a strict stop-loss, limit the number of sessions and fund them with money you can afford to lose. Return to your regular stakes if the shot fails.
