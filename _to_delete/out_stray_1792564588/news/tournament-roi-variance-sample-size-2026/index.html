Tournament ROI and Variance: How Many Events Is Enough?

A tournament player can be profitable and still lose for months. Learn how ROI, variance and sample size work, and what it means for your bankroll.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 19, 2026·8 min read

If you play multi-table tournaments, you have probably had a month where you played well and lost money. You may have also had a week where you played badly and finished in the money. Both experiences are completely normal, and they come from the same source: variance. Tournament poker pays out heavily at the top of the field and gives nothing to most entrants, so short-term results say very little about how good you are.

This guide explains return on investment, why sample size matters so much, and how to use that knowledge to protect your bankroll. It applies whether you are grinding the weekend majors or just starting out. What ROI actually measures

Return on investment, usually shortened to ROI, is your profit divided by the total buy-ins you have paid, including fees. If you have spent a thousand dollars across many events and you have won back eleven hundred, your profit is one hundred dollars and your ROI is ten percent.

Two details are worth remembering. First, ROI is a long-run average. It tells you what you would expect to earn per dollar invested over a large number of tournaments, not what will happen in any particular one. Second, fees count. A tournament with a ten percent fee means you must win back more than your buy-in before you break even at all, which is why tournament fees and rake deserve attention.

A modest positive ROI is a good result in most player pools. Large figures are usually produced by soft fields or small samples, and they tend to fall back toward the average over time.

Why tournament results are so volatile

In a cash game, a good player can win a pot most sessions. In a tournament, the structure works against steady results. Most of the prize pool goes to a small share of the field, often the top fifteen percent or fewer, and a large part of the money sits in the top few finishing places. A player who finishes in the money regularly can still make very little profit if they rarely reach the final table.

That means your results come mostly from a handful of big finishes. Remove one deep run from a year of play and many winning players would look like losing players. The reverse is also true: one large score can make an average player look like a star.

Imagine you play events with a field of a few hundred runners and finish in the money about one time in six. That means you lose your buy-in five times out of six, and you must rely on the occasional larger payout to make up the difference. A long cold streak is not a sign something is wrong. It is the expected shape of the results.

How big a sample do you need?

The honest answer is: more than you think. Measuring a winning ROI of a few percent against the natural swings of a tournament field typically takes thousands of events before the true figure becomes clear. Even a strong player with a double-digit ROI can run below that line over hundreds of tournaments.

Use a simple rule of thumb. If your sample is under a few hundred events, treat your results as mostly noise. If it is in the low thousands, you can start to draw tentative conclusions. Nothing short of that should convince you to change your stakes dramatically in either direction.

This is why serious players keep records and review them regularly. Our guide on tracking your poker results explains how to log sessions in a way that turns a pile of numbers into useful information.

Variance and your bankroll

Because tournament variance is high, bankroll requirements are higher than most beginners expect. A common guideline among experienced players is to hold many dozens of buy-ins for the level they play, and more for larger-field events. The exact figure depends on your skill edge, the field size, and how comfortable you are with long swings.

  • Small-field events have less variance and need fewer buy-ins.
  • Large-field and bounty events pay out more to fewer players, so they require a bigger cushion.
  • Satellites and sit and gos can lower variance, though they come with their own trade-offs.
  • Re-entry formats let you spend more per event, which increases your effective buy-in without you noticing.

Common mistakes when reading your own results

  • Judging a strategy after a few weeks. If you changed how you play and results got worse, the sample is almost certainly too small to prove anything.
  • Ignoring the effect of one big score. Always look at your results with and without your biggest finish.
  • Chasing losses by moving up. Taking a shot at bigger buy-ins after a downswing is the fastest way to turn a bad month into a bankroll crisis.
  • Forgetting fees and re-entries. ROI that leaves these out is inflated.
  • Comparing yourself with others. Published ROI figures usually come from selective samples.

Accepting these limits does not mean you can learn nothing from your results. It means you should lean on process measures as well as profit: whether you are making good decisions, reaching late stages often, and playing the right formats for your bankroll.

Practical steps to manage variance

You cannot remove variance from tournaments, but you can manage how it affects you. Start by setting a bankroll rule and sticking to it, even during good runs. Choose formats that suit your temperament; if long dry spells wear you down, mix in smaller fields or faster formats. Track every entry. And keep a separate budget for the occasional big event you want to play for fun.

It also helps to limit how many hours you play per session. Tilt grows when a long run of bad outcomes meets tired decision-making, so a cap on session length protects both your mood and your results. Our article on tilt control and session length goes through this in more detail.

The bottom line

A tournament player is judged over thousands of events, not dozens. If you understand ROI, respect variance, and hold a bankroll large enough to survive the bad stretches, you give your skill the time it needs to show up in the results. Everything else is noise.
