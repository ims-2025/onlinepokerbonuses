Is Online Poker Rigged? RNG, Bad Beats and the Truth

Think online poker is rigged? Learn how RNG shuffling works, why bad beats feel constant, and the real risks to watch for before you deposit anywhere.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 8, 2026·8 min read

Almost every online poker player has typed the same angry sentence into the chat box after losing with aces: this site is rigged. It is one of the most common beliefs in the game, and it has been around for as long as online poker itself. The truth is more interesting than a simple yes or no. Online hands do feel different from live ones, and there are real integrity concerns in poker, but they rarely involve the dealing of the cards the way players imagine.

This article explains how online shuffling works, why variance produces results that look suspicious, and where genuine risks actually live. Understanding the difference helps you pick safer places to play, and our best poker bonuses page is a good starting point for comparing rooms you can evaluate with a clear head.

How online poker shuffles the deck

Online poker rooms use a random number generator, or RNG, to shuffle a virtual deck before each hand. A well-built system draws on unpredictable entropy and applies a proper shuffling algorithm so that every possible ordering of the 52 cards is equally likely. The shuffle happens on the server, and your hole cards and the board are determined before anyone acts.

Because the deck is shuffled fresh every hand, there is no memory. The software does not know you lost three hands in a row, does not care how much you deposited, and has no reason to feed you bad cards. The idea that a site would script bad beats to keep players depositing is popular, but it would require the operator to create a pattern that regulators, auditors, and savvy mathematicians would eventually detect.

Why online poker feels more brutal than live

The biggest reason online hands feel rigged is volume. A live player might see 25 to 30 hands an hour. A regular online player at a single table sees 60 to 90, and anyone multi-tabling can see several hundred. More hands means you hit rare events more often. Running aces into kings, losing with a big flush to a bigger flush, or watching a one-outer land on the river simply happen more frequently when you play ten times the hands in the same time.

There is also a skill and speed effect. Online players call and chase draws more often than they would at a live table, partly because they play more tables and partly because a click is far less awkward than putting chips in the middle. More calls with weaker holdings produce more suckouts. That is not manipulation; it is the game being played at a different pace and with a looser population.

Finally, humans are poor at judging randomness. We remember painful losses far more vividly than routine wins, and we tend to expect results to alternate evenly. True random sequences contain long streaks, clusters, and droughts. If you think your results look streaky, you are probably just seeing normal variance, which is why variance math and downswing planning matters so much for bankroll management.

The math of bad beats

Take a classic example. Your pocket aces against pocket kings win roughly 80 percent of the time, so the kings win about one time in five. Over a few thousand hands, you will see that matchup multiple times, and you will lose it several times too. Similar logic applies to every standard all-in. A 70 percent favorite loses three times in ten, which is hardly unusual, yet it feels outrageous when it happens in a big pot.

The way to check yourself is to track results over a large sample. A few hundred hands tell you almost nothing. Tens of thousands begin to reflect your real win rate. Keeping records and reviewing them calmly is one of the best antidotes to tilt and conspiracy thinking, and it pairs well with tilt control and session length discipline.

Where real integrity risks actually live

Calling the cards rigged is usually wrong, but poker does have genuine integrity problems that deserve attention. They are about the players and the operators, not the shuffle.

  • Collusion. Two or more players sharing information to take advantage of others is a real risk, and strong rooms invest in detection tools and manual review.
  • Bots. Automated players can grind stakes at low cost. Reputable rooms use behavioral analysis and report removals, as we discuss in our piece on bots and game integrity.
  • Ghosting and real-time assistance. Using outside help while playing a hand violates the rules at nearly every site.
  • Operator trustworthiness. The bigger question for offshore players is whether the company will pay out, handle disputes fairly, and protect player funds.
  • Insider abuse. Historically, a few well-known scandals involved employees or affiliated players seeing hole cards, which prompted tighter security practices industry-wide.

Notice that none of those involve the software dealing losing hands on purpose. They are about trust in the people and the company behind the table, which is exactly why operator reputation is worth more attention than conspiracy theories about the shuffle.

How to vet a poker room before you deposit

You cannot audit a shuffle yourself, but you can evaluate the room around it. Look at how long the operator has been running, whether payout complaints appear consistently, how withdrawals are handled, and whether the site publishes clear terms. A long track record of paying players matters far more than any promise about fairness.

Our walkthrough on evaluating a poker room before depositing lays out a practical checklist, and our individual reviews, such as the Americas Cardroom review and the BetOnline Poker review, summarize what we know about banking, software, and bonuses at each room.

It also helps to start small. Make a modest first deposit, play a few sessions, request a small withdrawal, and see how the cashier treats you before moving a larger bankroll. This is the single most reliable way to test whether a room behaves the way it promises.

What to do when you suspect something is wrong

If you believe you have seen cheating, such as obvious collusion, a suspicious player, or software that seems to be behaving oddly, gather evidence first. Save hand histories, note table names and times, and contact support with specifics instead of general accusations. Reputable rooms investigate reports, and a clear, factual message gets taken far more seriously than an angry one.

If you are simply running badly, the healthiest response is to step away, review your play, and drop down in stakes if the swings are hurting your bankroll. The cards are random, but your decisions are not, and decisions are the one thing you control.

The bottom line

Online poker is not rigged in the sense most frustrated players mean. The shuffle is random, the volume is high, and variance is brutal. The real risks sit around collusion, bots, and the trustworthiness of the operator, and those are things you can reduce by choosing carefully and keeping your bankroll manageable.

Frequently asked questions

Can an online poker site control which cards I get?

Properly run rooms use a random number generator to shuffle each hand independently, so there is no memory of past results or player balances. Bad beats feel frequent mostly because online players see many more hands per hour than live players.

What are the real cheating risks in online poker?

The main concerns are collusion between players, automated bots, outside assistance during hands, and operator trustworthiness. These are best managed by choosing established rooms, starting with small deposits, and testing withdrawals early.
