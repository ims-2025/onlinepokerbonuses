Delayed C-Bet Strategy: Check the Flop, Bet the Turn

Learn when checking back the flop and betting the turn beats a standard continuation bet, with board textures, sizing and common mistakes explained.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 13, 2026·8 min read

Most players learn the continuation bet early: raise preflop, get called, bet the flop whether or not you hit. It is a sound default, but it is only a default. A player who c-bets every flop becomes predictable, and a predictable player is easy to play against. The delayed continuation bet, where you raise preflop, check back the flop in position and then bet the turn, is one of the simplest ways to add balance and extract value without learning anything exotic.

This guide covers what a delayed c-bet is, which flops and hands suit it, how to size the turn bet, and the mistakes that turn a good idea into a leak. Whether you are grinding cash games to clear a welcome offer from our best poker bonuses list or just trying to win more at the tables, the concepts apply at every stake.

What a delayed c-bet actually is

A delayed c-bet is a bet made on the turn by the preflop aggressor after the flop checked through. There are two common versions. In the first, you are out of position as the raiser, you check the flop, your opponent checks behind, and you bet the turn. In the second, which is far more common and more profitable, you are in position as the raiser, your opponent checks to you, you check back the flop, and then bet the turn when your opponent checks again.

The key point is that the flop check is a deliberate choice, not a default. You are giving up a small amount of immediate fold equity on the flop in exchange for a better situation on the turn: more information, a more favourable pot size and often a range advantage that is more obvious to you than to your opponent.

Why checking back the flop can be right

Betting the flop with your entire range is not optimal on every board. There are four recurring reasons to check back instead.

  • Protection against check-raises. On boards where your opponent's range contains many strong draws and mid-pair hands, a flop bet invites raises that put your marginal hands in awkward spots. Checking avoids that.
  • Pot control with medium-strength hands. Hands such as second pair or a weak top pair often want to reach showdown cheaply. Checking back lets them do so and still collect on later streets when the opponent is weak.
  • Inducing bluffs and weak bets. Opponents who see a check often take a stab on the turn. If you are strong enough to call or raise, that is free value.
  • Setting up a bigger turn bet. Checking the flop makes the pot smaller, but a well-sized turn bet from a range that now looks capped on the flop can still force folds from hands that would have called a small flop bet.

Which boards favour a delayed c-bet

Board texture is the starting point. Delayed c-bets work best on boards that connect well with the defender's calling range but where your range still has strong holdings and good turn cards.

  • Wet, middling boards. Something like 8-7-6 or 9-8-4 with two suits hits many of the hands that call a raise. A flop bet gets raised or called often, and checking back keeps the pot manageable.
  • Low connected boards when you hold overcards. If you raised ace-king or ace-queen and the flop is low and coordinated, checking back keeps your equity and lets you bet when an ace or king arrives.
  • Paired boards in position. On boards like Q-Q-5 or 9-9-3 your opponent has few strong hands, but a bet often folds out only what you beat. Checking back with some medium hands and betting later can extract more.

By contrast, dry, high boards such as K-7-2 rainbow favour a simple, high-frequency flop c-bet because your opponent's range is weak and your range has a clear advantage. Checking everything there throws away easy profit. Our guide to wet versus dry boards is a useful companion if you want to sharpen texture reading.

Which hands to check back

A balanced delayed c-bet strategy includes several hand types, so that your flop check does not shout weakness or strength:

  1. Medium-strength made hands. Second pair, weak top pair and pocket pairs below the board want to see a turn without building a big pot.
  2. Strong hands you want to disguise. Occasionally checking a set or two pair on a wet board lets you build the pot with a bigger turn bet while keeping weaker hands in.
  3. Draws with showdown value or good turn equity. Hands like gutshots with an overcard can improve on the turn and often make good semi-bluffs.
  4. Air with good blockers. A few pure bluff candidates, particularly hands that block the opponent's strong continuing range, balance the range.

Notice what is missing: you do not need to check back every weak hand and bet every strong one. Mixing the categories is the point, because that is what keeps opponents guessing.

Sizing the turn bet

Because the flop was checked, the pot is smaller than usual, and your turn sizing should reflect that. A bet of roughly half to two thirds of the pot works well in most spots, and larger sizes make sense on wet turns where your opponent has many draws to charge. Very small bets are usually a mistake after a flop check, since opponents with a pair or draw can call cheaply and realise their equity.

Think too about the next street. If you plan to bet the river as well, make sure your turn size leaves a sensible stack-to-pot ratio. A turn bet that leaves you with a tiny remaining stack commits you to shoving the river with hands that cannot stand a call.

Good turn cards for a delayed c-bet

The turn card matters as much as the flop. Cards that improve your perceived range while hurting the opponent's give you the best chance of success:

  • Overcards to the board. An ace or king on a middling board hits your raising range more often than a caller's range.
  • Cards that complete missed draws on your side. If your range includes the flush or straight draws that arrived, a bet carries more credibility.
  • Blanks after an opponent check. A harmless turn after two checks suggests your opponent has little, and a bet often takes down the pot.

Be cautious when the turn pairs the board in a way that favours the caller or completes obvious draws against you. A cool-headed check again, followed by a pot-controlled showdown, is often the better line.

Common mistakes

Players who experiment with delayed c-bets tend to repeat the same errors:

  • Checking back the entire range. The strategy works because it is selective. If every check back is followed by a turn bet, strong opponents will start raising your bets.
  • Betting too small on the turn. Small bets give away good odds to draws and floats.
  • Ignoring the opponent. Against calling stations, you should bet more for value and bluff less. Against players who fold too much to turn pressure, increase your bluff frequency. Our guide to exploiting calling stations shows how to adjust.
  • Using it out of position too often. A delayed c-bet is cleanest in position. Out of position, checking the flop gives the opponent a free chance to take control.

Putting it into practice

Start small. Pick one board type, such as wet middling flops in position, and decide in advance which hands you will check back and which turns you will bet. Track the results over a few thousand hands before expanding the idea. Frequently asked questions

What is a delayed continuation bet in poker?

It is a turn bet made by the preflop raiser after the flop checked through. The raiser deliberately checks back the flop, then bets the turn to win the pot or extract value.

When should I avoid a delayed c-bet?

Avoid it on dry boards where a flop c-bet is clearly profitable, and when the turn card favours your opponent. Also be careful against opponents who rarely fold on later streets.
