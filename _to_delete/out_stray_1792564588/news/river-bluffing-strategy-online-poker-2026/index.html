River Bluffing Strategy: When to Fire the Last Bet

Learn when river bluffs make money in online poker: choosing bluff candidates, sizing, reading the story your line tells, and spots to give up.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 17, 2026·8 min read

The river is where poker stops being about equity and becomes about decisions. There are no more cards to come, so every hand is either a value hand, a bluff-catcher or air. Bluffing the river well is one of the clearest separators between players who break even and players who win, and it is also one of the easiest ways to light money on fire. This guide covers when a river bluff makes sense, how to choose the right hands to bluff with, and how to size the bet.

Online play makes the skill more valuable. You see many more river decisions per hour than a live player, and small leaks multiply quickly. The maths behind a river bluff

A bluff is profitable when the opponent folds often enough to cover the risk. If you bet half the pot, you need your opponent to fold more than one time in three for a pure bluff to break even. If you bet the full pot, you need folds about half the time. If you overbet, the required fold rate climbs further.

That simple ratio is the starting point for every decision. The question is never whether a bluff can work. It is whether the opponent in front of you folds often enough, given the story your line has told. A pot-sized bluff against someone who never folds a pair is a donation; the same bet against a tight player holding a weak showdown hand may be excellent.

Choose bluffs with the right blockers

Not every missed draw is a good bluff. The best candidates are hands that cannot win at showdown and that remove some of the strong hands your opponent could be holding. Holding the ace of the suit on a three-flush board blocks the nut flush, which makes it less likely your opponent can call profitably and more likely they are weak. Likewise, holding a card that completes a straight can make it harder for the opponent to have it.

  • Missed draws that block value. An ace-high flush draw that bricked is the classic example: no showdown value, and you hold a key card.
  • Hands with no showdown value. If your hand can win by checking, it is usually a bluff-catcher or check-back, not a bluff.
  • Hands that unblock folds. Cards that do not remove the weaker hands you want your opponent to fold make your bluff more credible.

The opposite also matters. Avoid bluffing with hands that have some showdown value, since those are often better as checks that can win against missed draws. A pair of nines on a flush-completing board is a bluff-catcher, not a bluff.

Tell a believable story

River bluffs work best when your whole line makes sense. If you called the flop and turn passively, then suddenly fire a pot-sized bet on a blank river, a thoughtful opponent will wonder why you did not bet earlier with your strong hands. If you raised preflop, continuation bet and barrelled the turn on a board that favours your range, a river bet on a scare card tells a coherent story.

Scare cards and range advantage

Scare cards are river cards that improve your range more than your opponent's. When an ace or king arrives on a board where you raised preflop, your opponent has fewer of those cards, and you can credibly represent them. A card that completes an obvious draw works the same way if you plausibly held that draw. These are the best rivers to continue your story with a bluff.

Equally important is the board that gets worse for you. If the river pairs the board or completes a draw your line would not have taken, giving up is usually correct. Know your opponent before you bluff

Population tendencies matter. At low stakes, many players call too often with any pair, and river bluffs lose value against them. At mid stakes, players fold more, and a well-chosen bluff is often profitable. You should bluff less against calling stations and more against tight, thoughtful opponents who fold marginal hands.

Look for these tells in your opponent's earlier play:

  • Passive lines. Opponents who check-call both streets often have capped, medium-strength hands that can fold to a big river bet.
  • Quick calls on earlier streets. A fast call often signals a drawing or marginal hand, not a monster.
  • Small bets that stalled. If your opponent bet small and then gave up, their range is often weak and will fold to pressure.
  • History of showdowns. If they have repeatedly shown down weak hands, they may be willing to call light; if they rarely do, they are more foldable.

Pick the right size

River sizing is a lever that changes both the fold equity you need and the hands that can call. A smaller bluff, around a third to half the pot, needs fewer folds to work but also gets called by more hands. A larger bluff, around the size of the pot or more, needs more folds but can push out hands that would call a small bet.

Use the same size for your bluffs and your value bets in the same spot. If you only bet big with strong hands and small with bluffs, a good opponent will notice. Balance matters less against the weakest opponents, but it protects you against stronger ones as you move up.

When to give up

Giving up is a skill. Folding or checking behind is correct when the opponent is unlikely to fold, when your hand has showdown value, or when the board does not support your story. A disciplined player skips more bluffs than they fire, and that restraint is part of what makes the bluffs they do take work.

Tracking your results helps. Review the river bluffs you fired, how often they worked, and which opponents folded. Over a few thousand hands patterns emerge, and you can tighten the spots that lose money. Bankroll and tilt considerations

River bluffs are high-variance plays. Each one risks a large share of the pot, and a few failed attempts in a row can tempt you to chase. Keep your bankroll comfortable for your stakes, take a break after a bad run, and avoid bluffing out of frustration. A calm session with fewer, better bluffs beats an impulsive one.

The bottom line

Good river bluffs combine the right maths, the right blockers, a believable story and the right opponent. Start by choosing a few high-quality spots rather than bluffing often, study the results, and adjust to how your opponent pool actually behaves. Over time the extra folds you collect become a meaningful source of profit.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I bluff the river?

Bluff less often against players who call too much and more against thoughtful opponents who fold marginal hands. Pick a few high-quality spots with good blockers rather than bluffing frequently.

What size should a river bluff be?

Half to full pot is common. Smaller bets need fewer folds but get called more; larger bets need more folds but can push out stronger hands. Use similar sizes for value and bluffs.
