Is Poker Skill or Chance? US & Canada Law Explained

Courts and lawmakers disagree on whether poker is a game of skill or chance. See the legal tests used in the US and Canada and what they mean for players.

OnlinePokerBonuses Editorial Team·Published October 9, 2026·8 min read

Few debates in poker are as old as this one: is the game one of skill or one of chance? Players who spend years studying ranges and bet sizing tend to say skill, pointing to the fact that the best players win consistently over large samples. Lawmakers and judges, however, have to apply statutes that were often written long before online poker existed, and the answer they reach can differ from one place to the next. Understanding how the question is framed helps explain why the legal landscape looks the way it does in the United States and Canada.

This article is general information, not legal advice, and laws change. If you have a specific question, consult a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction. For the practical side of where players can sit down today, our overviews for the USA and Canada are the best places to start.

Why the skill-versus-chance question matters

Most gambling laws in North America define gambling in terms of three elements: consideration (you pay to play), a prize, and chance. If a game is classified as one where chance is the deciding factor, it generally falls within gambling prohibitions unless it is licensed or specifically exempted. If skill is dominant, some jurisdictions treat it differently, which has been the basis of many legal arguments over the years for poker, fantasy sports and certain other contests.

Poker sits in an uncomfortable middle. Cards are dealt at random, so short-term outcomes depend heavily on luck. Over thousands of hands, decisions about position, pot odds and bluffing make a measurable difference. Courts have to decide which of these facts the statute cares about, and the wording of the statute usually decides the outcome.

The three main legal tests in the United States

American states generally use one of three approaches, although the exact wording and the way courts apply them vary widely.

  • Dominant factor (or predominance) test: a game counts as gambling if chance, rather than skill, is the dominant factor in determining the outcome. This is the most common standard and, in theory, gives poker its best argument.
  • Material element test: a game counts as gambling if chance plays a material role, even if skill is also involved. This test is much harder on poker, because luck clearly matters in every hand.
  • Any chance test: a stricter approach that treats a game as gambling if any chance element is present. Under this test, almost any card game would qualify.

The same game can therefore be treated differently across state lines. A court applying the dominant factor test might be open to arguments about skill, while one applying a stricter standard may not be. In practice, many states have settled the matter through statutes rather than court rulings, either by explicitly licensing poker or by listing it among prohibited games.

Notable court decisions and why they did not settle it

Over the years, several US courts have examined poker. Some trial courts have been receptive to the idea that skill predominates, while appellate courts have often reversed or narrowed those decisions, focusing instead on the specific language of the statute. The takeaway is that no single case has settled the issue for the whole country. Even where a judge has accepted that skill matters a great deal, the legal consequence depends on whether the law asks about the dominant factor, a material element or any chance at all.

Federal law adds another layer. Statutes such as the Wire Act and the Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act deal with interstate gambling businesses and payment processing rather than directly criminalizing a player at a table, and interpretations have shifted over time. Our explainer on the Wire Act and UIGEA walks through what those laws do and do not cover for players.

How states responded: regulation instead of definition

Rather than resolve the philosophical question, a handful of states chose to license and regulate online poker directly, which sidesteps the skill-versus-chance debate entirely. Regulated markets exist in several states, and some have linked player pools through interstate compacts. Others have no licensed online option at all, and offshore sites accept players from many of those states, operating under foreign licenses. The legal status of the players themselves differs by state, and most enforcement history focuses on operators rather than individual recreational players.

The Canadian approach

Canada handles the question differently. The Criminal Code of Canada defines and restricts gaming and betting at the federal level, but it delegates authority over licensing and conducting gambling to provinces. In practice, each province decides what is offered within its borders, whether through provincial lotteries and casinos or through licensed private operators.

Ontario created a regulated market for private online operators, and other provinces run their own platforms or maintain more restrictive approaches. The legal framework is less about whether poker is skill or chance and more about who is authorized to operate and offer games to residents. Players in provinces without a regulated private market often use international sites, and the legal position of those sites is a matter for the province and federal law to interpret. What this means for players

For most recreational players, the skill-versus-chance debate matters less than a few practical questions: where do I live, what does the law in my state or province say, and which sites can I access? A few habits can keep you on solid ground.

  • Check the current rules where you live before depositing, because laws and enforcement priorities change.
  • Use licensed, regulated options where they exist, and understand that offshore sites operate under different consumer protections.
  • Keep records of deposits, withdrawals and results, which also helps with tax reporting.
  • Avoid tools that hide your location, since many sites prohibit them and they can put your balance at risk.

Taxes are a separate matter. Winnings can be reportable depending on where you live, and the rules for casual players and professionals differ. Our guide to whether poker bonuses are taxable is a good primer, but a tax professional can give advice specific to your situation.

The bottom line

Poker is a game in which skill and chance both play real roles, and the law has never fully agreed on how to weigh them. In the United States, the outcome depends on the state and the statute, and in Canada it depends on provincial authorization within a federal framework. That patchwork is why two players with the same skill level can face different rules depending on where they live.

Frequently asked questions

Is poker legally considered a game of skill in the United States?

It depends on the state. Some use a dominant factor test that leaves room for skill arguments, while others use a material element or any chance test, and many simply regulate or prohibit poker by statute.

Does Canada treat poker as skill or chance?

Canadian law focuses less on that question and more on who is authorized to offer gaming. The Criminal Code sets the framework, and provinces license or operate games, including online poker, within their borders.
